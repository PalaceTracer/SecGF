# 03 — Pulse, percussion, bass, and groove

## The decision this chapter supports

Describe how the recording moves, not merely how many beats it has per minute. A useful rhythm instruction lets a listener predict where the weight falls, how the small attacks behave between beats, how bass and percussion cooperate, and what changes when a section opens up. The finished lyric supplies the words; rhythm decisions should make their stresses and breaths workable. A Style prompt rarely needs every term below. It needs the decisive relationships for this particular song, within the character budget. These are written design proposals, not guaranteed Suno commands. The writer works from text only and does not receive or evaluate audio.

## Pulse versus perceived speed

**Pulse** is the recurring beat a listener could tap. **Tempo** describes the rate of that pulse. **Perceived speed** also depends on the rate and force of the attacks inside each beat, phrase length, bass note duration, and the placement of backbeats. A track at a fixed tempo can feel broad when the kick and snare state large intervals while a sustained bass spans them; it can feel urgent when short hats and bass notes mark subdivisions. Do not infer tempo from how many syllables the lyric contains. A dense line can be delivered rapidly over a broad pulse, while a sparse line can float across an active pattern.

Use exact BPM when the current user brief supplies it or when a precise rate is musically decisive. A number in an enjoyed reference describes that example and does not set this song's tempo. Otherwise choose a verbal range that conveys motion: “unhurried, weighty pulse”; “brisk pulse with short, even subdivisions”; “moderate pace with an expansive half-time backbeat.” A deliberately chosen BPM is a composition proposal, not a measured reference fact; never present a guessed reference tempo as verified. The songwriter never receives audio to measure tempo. No audio measurement is an input to this workflow.

The apparent **half-time** or **double-time** relationship is a perception of accent spacing, not necessarily a tempo change. “Fast small percussion over a broad half-time snare” tells the generator which layer carries urgency and which layer carries weight. “Double-time feel” without a layer named can produce wholesale speeding. Ask which body movement should dominate: head nod, quick step, long sway, or continuous drive. That is an internal diagnostic, not a phrase that must be pasted into Style.

An **every-beat kick** gives a low anchor on each beat; a **half-time backbeat** spreads the strong counter-accent across a broader interval. These can appear in alternating sections at one tempo, or coexist when their roles are clear. Name the change in perceived weight, optionally alongside a useful genre/style descriptor; a reference label alone does not establish the pattern: “The verse keeps spacious kick placements and a broad snare; the refrain puts a firm kick on every beat while its upper subdivisions stay brisk.” Choose this contrast only when the lyric's arrival benefits from it. A long narrative verse can also use every-beat anchors with thin accompaniment, and a refrain can retain broad accents.

## Meter, subdivision, and recurring placement

**Meter** groups beats into repeating cycles; **subdivision** splits a beat into smaller placements. A listener may hear a regular four-beat frame, a three-beat turn, or a deliberately uneven cycle. Choose grouping by the brief and compatible phrase design, with the same musical-fit standard for familiar and unfamiliar meters. Specify it when the detail matters; unfamiliarity alone is not a reason to reject it. For a short prompt, “four-beat pulse” or “rolling three-beat cycle” often communicates more than a fraction alone.

**Straight** subdivisions divide the beat evenly. **Swung** subdivisions give paired attacks unequal durations, typically lengthening the first. Avoid treating “swing” as a single percentage or as a proxy for looseness. The subdivision may be unequal while the kick and backbeat stay emphatically regular. A line such as “even kick anchors; lightly loping hats” identifies the relationship. If the desired lilt is confined to the bass or vocal, name that layer. Conversely, “straight, even eighth-note percussion” is a sensible positive specification when the current brief requires firm spacing.

**Backbeat** names a recurring accent that counters or answers the main pulse. It can be heavy, restrained, delayed in feel, sparse, or doubled. “Hard backbeat” may imply louder impact, but not where the kick falls. “Sparse kick under a firm backbeat” is more precise. A faint backbeat can let the bass line carry momentum; a heavy one can create perceived width and certainty even at a brisk tempo. The question is not “does the song need a backbeat?” but “which layer tells the listener where a phrase lands?”

**Syncopation** shifts emphasis toward weaker or unexpected positions relative to an established meter. It needs a stable reference to register as displacement. Ask what remains predictable while another layer moves against it. A bass anticipation before a downbeat may provide forward pull; snare accents that miss every expected point may instead obscure the frame. Experimental work found that moderate syncopation could increase movement ratings in controlled rhythm examples, while too much reduced them, so consider it a limited finding about predictability rather than a universal ranking. [Witek and colleagues’ original experiment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3989225/) and [a related controlled experiment](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01036/full) are evidence for the interaction between predictability and displacement, not evidence that one pattern suits every song.

## Event density, space, and the pocket

**Event density** is the number of attacks over a span, separate from tempo and loudness. Quiet rapid hats can make a moderate pulse feel busy; a large but infrequent kick can feel spacious.

**Pocket** is a listener’s sense that layers sit coherently around a repeated pulse. It includes timing, articulation, and mutual space. It is not synonymous with random timing errors. **Push** suggests a layer articulates toward the front of the perceived beat; **laid-back** suggests a layer sits toward its rear; **centered** suggests attacks align closely with it. These are intended relative timing descriptions, not reliable millisecond specifications for text-to-audio. If a vocal should press against a steady backing, say so explicitly: “steady percussion; lead phrases lean forward.” If the drum kit should feel relaxed while the bass anticipates entrances, assign those roles separately. Do not stack “loose, tight, raw, perfectly quantized” in one instruction.

Do not default to quantized = lifeless or microtimed = alive. A performed sound may carry life through dynamic accents, note lengths, ghost notes, and changing tone while staying close to a grid. Conversely, exaggerated lateness can make a groove feel uncertain. The [microtiming experiment](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01487/full) supports avoiding a blanket direction to displace all events. For Style, describe the audible outcome: “precise pulse with gently varied hat accents,” “unhurried backbeat against punctual bass,” or “firm, interlocking attacks.” The exact production mechanism is less important than the intended relationship.

## Percussion as functions, not a shopping list

Percussion instructions are strongest when each sound has a job:

| Function | Audible question | Possible compact wording |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ground | Which low attack defines the beat or phrase landing? | “Short, centered kick marks the pulse.” |
| Counterweight | Which midrange hit gives a recurring answer? | “Dry snare answers on a broad backbeat.” |
| Subdivision | Which lighter attacks create motion between main beats? | “Fine, even hats create urgency above spacious drums.” |
| Accent | Which rare hit marks an important word or turn? | “Small metallic accents punctuate section endings.” |
| Transition | What announces the next section? | “A short rising fill gives way to a clean downbeat.” |
| Absence | Which expected hit disappears to create a gap? | “One-beat drum break exposes the final word.” |

## Bass: rhythmic role, note length, and register

Bass connects rhythm and harmony. It can reinforce roots, trace a countermelody, hold a pedal tone while chords change, anticipate a downbeat, or leave a deliberate hole around a kick. Possible bass responsibilities include **anchor**, **motor**, **reply**, and **drone**; they may combine, change by passage, or be absent. An anchor joins large accents and sustains the harmonic center. A motor makes frequent short attacks. A reply enters the gaps after another part. A drone persists through changing upper harmony. These are functions, not sound-source categories.

**Note length** often matters as much as note choice. A short bass decay clears room for the next kick and preserves articulation. A long, smooth note gives continuity but may conceal small kick attacks if both occupy the same low range. “Round bass notes that end before the next kick” is a useful relationship. “Bass blooms after each kick” suggests an audible swell, but do not assume a specific compression effect will be generated. If the need is only clarity, specify the result rather than a processing chain. [Ableton’s audio-effect reference](https://www.ableton.com/en/live-manual/12/live-audio-effect-reference/) documents one production method for making low elements yield to a kick; the prompt should normally describe the audible interaction, not prescribe a plugin setting.

**Kick–bass alignment** has several valid designs. They can strike together for a single large impact; the bass can arrive just before the kick to pull the phrase forward; it can enter after the kick to create a two-part motion; or it can fall in the spaces between kicks. Specify the relevant relation by passage; a deliberate change or combined low-end role is valid when its timing is clear. Saying “syncopated bass over a steady kick” is useful when the rhythm must move without destabilizing the beat. Saying “kick and bass lock” is too vague if a particular pattern of exchange is crucial. Low-frequency overlap can obscure both elements; [iZotope’s engineering demonstration](https://www.izotope.com/community/blog/how-to-mix-kick-and-bass) illustrates why making room in frequency, duration, or arrangement can improve clarity. This is a design principle, not a claim that a text prompt gives surgical frequency control.

## Low-end envelope: what begins and what remains

Attack, sustain, and release describe different parts of the low-end motion. Decide which source announces the beat and which provides continuing weight. A round long sub can coexist with a short hard kick if its onset or level leaves room for the kick. A highly clipped bass can create audible pitch replies without sustaining the whole space. Saturation, compression, and a particular bass-source name do not themselves establish this relationship.

| Design | Intended relationship | Original Style fragment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shared impact | Kick and low note begin together; low note has controlled duration | “Kick and bass strike the phrase landing together; bass ends before the next low attack.” |
| Kick then sustain | Kick supplies the transient; bass blooms afterward | “A short kick opens each beat, followed by a smooth low swell.” |
| Stable anchor and reply | Kick keeps its repeating pattern; bass occupies selected gaps | “Regular kicks hold the frame while clipped bass notes answer between vocal phrases.” |
| Sustained low floor | Long note bridges sparse percussion; upper bass edge can supply definition | “One low note spans the line beneath sparse dry kicks.” |
| Momentary low-end yielding | Sub level dips at the kick and returns afterward | “Sub briefly yields to each kick, then rises smoothly into the space after it.” |

These are options. Every song does not need ducked sub, deep sustained bass, or a fixed low-frequency range. A short prompt can communicate an envelope without naming devices, exact compressor settings, or cutoff numbers. A technique supplied by the current brief may be retained when it adds useful precision; a device mentioned only in a liked prompt should be translated into perceptible behavior or omitted. Choose the bass–voice relation: simultaneous independent lines can use distinct registers, durations or staggered phrase peaks; alternating replies can occupy gaps. Neither timing relation is the default definition of clarity.

## Breaks, subdivision changes, and abrupt silence

Use a transition to mark something in the finished lyric: a question ending, repeated vow, change of addressee, withheld answer, or last sung word. First identify the lyric event, then decide whether the arrangement needs anticipation, interruption, a weight change, or withdrawal. A reference's buildup/drop labels do not add those sections to a different lyric. A proposed instrumental break belongs in the private plan and can be named in Style when authorized by the current brief; leave the finished lyrics intact.

**Subdivision change:** smaller attacks can become more frequent over the same large beat. “Upper ticks tighten toward the return while kick spacing stays fixed” separates felt tension from a tempo ramp. The reverse move can open room for a long sung vowel. Increasing attack rate on every layer can obscure the beat and text; assign the change to the layer doing the transition.

**Riser or fill:** a short upward swell or tightening percussion figure can announce a return. Choose one clear function and end it before the new impact. “A thin rising noise ends at the cut” differs from “a drum fill carries through the entrance.” Neither is a universal requirement for an electronic passage. Use an exact subdivision such as sixteenths only when its rate matters enough to justify the detail.

**Break:** removing drums can expose voice, acoustic grain, or the existing hook. Choose what continues through the drum break: full sustained ensemble, independent bass, exposed voice or another authorized texture. Retain definite verse melody in a sung passage. Sparse backing does not require a spoken lead. A break can retain bass or residual room sound; name the surviving role when it makes the contrast work.

**Micro-silence:** define its scope and location. An all-sound cut stops direct sources and effect tails; a percussion stop leaves the other material continuous. Put a brief gap after the lead's completed vowel if the word should remain intact. “Cut all layers for half a beat after the last word, then restore the low pulse beneath the hook” makes the interruption's duration and function clear. A beat fraction is a design choice, not a default or guaranteed sample-accurate control. Repeated cuts may fragment dense singing, so select a few meaningful locations.

**Return:** decide what survives and what expands. The same pulse may return with longer bass notes, a higher hook carrier, wider chords, or more frequent light attacks. One decisive change can communicate an arrival. Do not prescribe all of them in every song. **Withdrawal:** removing attacks while a melody or chord sustains can provide an ending distinct from another large arrival; specify whether the last tail rings out or cuts.

## Shared time, meter and phrase span

The beat need not be a permanent drum grid. Distinguish a flexible lead over strict support, a whole ensemble following local sung timing, and a passage with no recurring beat. These are different relationships. In singer-led flexible time, accompaniment can wait through a held vowel and share the next onset. Without recurring pulse, melodic contour, breath, text or harmonic arrival can organize duration. Pitched singing remains definite; free timing does not imply speech. Do not add drums solely to complete a rhythm clause.

Meter groups beats; subdivision describes smaller units. A triple cycle can place low weight on its first beat and lighter chord attacks on the following beats. A compound grouping divides its principal beats into threes; it is not merely “faster triple meter.” An additive grouping combines unequal beat groups into a repeating span. Choose numerical groupings only when decisive; they are proposed composition details, not measurements inferred from a catalog label.

Phrase span is independent of meter. A melodic arch may cross several regular bars; a short accompaniment cell can restart across bar lines before a shared landing. **Syncopation** displaces emphasis within a frame; **metric displacement** can preserve a figure while shifting its alignment against that frame. Neither necessarily changes tempo. Alternating meters change the grouping itself. Do not stack all three devices to imply sophistication.

Original single-relationship fragments: “The pitched phrase crosses the bar while low accents mark the steady frame”; “Piano waits through the sung cadence before both start the next phrase”; “A short upper pattern crosses successive bar lines, with a shared landing at the completed thought.” Adapt only when the brief leaves that choice open. Unequal phrase lengths must accommodate the unchanged words, not force extra syllables or repeated text.

## Several layers of motion

A recurring broken-drum cycle can define movement without a kick on each beat. Sustained singing may span its fast attacks; long bass notes can provide a slower frame, or clipped bass can join selected drum events. Fast percussion does not require fast words, a new tempo or speech. Conversely, a repeating low pattern can carry pulse while upper instruments and voice remain rhythmically independent.

Straight and unequal subdivisions can coexist when assigned deliberately to different sources or separate passages; a global “straight and swung throughout” leaves the time relation ambiguous. If phrase-following passages alternate with strict interludes, state their boundary and the cue that reconnects them. Do not invent interludes where the actual lyric or brief gives no space.

## Bass has pitch, rhythm and responsibility

Bass can articulate a harmonic root, connect chords, sustain a pedal or carry an independent tune. Its harmonic destination does not determine its kick alignment. Keep a clipped onset pattern while changing a selected refrain landing to root support, or preserve pitch while changing note lengths. A root-position chord is not necessarily tonic closure. If an upper common tone supplies continuity, bass can move or shorten beneath it; a foreground bass motif need not double every kick.

If the arrangement is pulse-free or unaccompanied, no kick–bass relationship is required. If bass and sung melody jointly imply harmony, continuous chord pads may be unnecessary. This is an available organization, not a rule to make every song economical. These original implementations generalize questions in the separately retrievable catalog, not measurements or source reconstructions.

## Diagnostic questions before compiling

1. Does this design need a stable beat, shared flexible timing, or no recurring pulse?
2. Does the desired urgency come from the beat rate, from subdivisions, from bass articulation, or from vocal phrasing?
3. Which sound tells the listener where the large beat lands? Which one moves against it?
4. Is the backbeat strong, restrained, absent, or section-dependent?
5. Does the lyric need a gap for a breath, a name, or a decisive final word?
6. Should the bass sound primarily as weight, audible pitch, rhythmic edge, or melodic reply?
7. Do kick and bass coincide, alternate, or overlap with a clear hierarchy?
8. Does the actual form need growth, stasis, transformation, withdrawal or another relation?
9. Does a transition change beat rate, accent spacing, or only the smaller attacks?
10. If there is a silence, which sources and tails stop, for how long, and after which completed lyric event?

## Prompt phrasing: from vague to audible

The following are original, anonymous phrasing examples. They describe intended audible results, not verified Suno behavior. The songwriter does not hear generated files.

| Vague request | More specific Style fragment | Intended audible result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Make it fast and powerful.” | “Brisk, even pulse; sparse low kick and hard backbeat under rapid, quiet hats.” | Strong large beats coexist with small high attacks. |
| “Groovy bass.” | “Short, rounded bass notes answer the kick between downbeats.” | Bass entries fill gaps rather than duplicating the kick. |
| “Human drums.” | “Firm beat with varied soft upper-percussion accents and few fills.” | Pulse remains stable; upper layer has dynamic differences. |
| “Big chorus.” | “Refrain widens with longer bass notes and denser hats; the core beat stays unchanged.” | Section changes in duration and density without speed drift. |
| “Swinging beat.” | “Even kick anchors a lightly loping subdivision in the upper percussion.” | Unequal small-note spacing sits over a stable low pulse. |
| “No clutter.” | “Percussion and sung phrases retain distinct attack patterns within continuous ensemble motion.” | Intended attacks remain identifiable; reduced density is another option, not a requirement. |
| “Huge drop.” | “After the last sung vowel, a half-beat all-sound cut precedes the returning every-beat kick and doubled hook.” | Interrupted continuity makes a specific return conspicuous. |
| “Fast verses.” | “Short syllabic notes follow a recurring pitched verse motif over broad drum accents.” | Vocal articulation is quick while the main rhythmic weight stays spacious; the verse remains sung. |
| “Acoustic electronic blend.” | “Dry hand-played clicks mark the small pulse; a round low swell answers the electronic kick.” | Acoustic and electronic sources have distinct jobs within one groove. |
