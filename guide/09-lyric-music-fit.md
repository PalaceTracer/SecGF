# 09 — Lyric–music fit

## Analyze words without rewriting them

The Grok lyric generator supplies finished text; this chapter designs its delivery. Words go in Lyrics, decisive production cues in Style. [Suno Custom Mode](https://help.suno.com/en/articles/2415873/) documents the separate lyric field. The wording examples below are **listening hypotheses**.

Read aloud. Mark syllable density, natural stress, complete thoughts, repetition, and turns. Which words must be caught on first play? Where would a breath or strong beat distort the meaning?

| Lyric property | Musical choice to consider | Test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dense new information | Short, mostly syllabic notes over a stable pulse; less overlap. | Can an uninformed listener catch key words? |
| A short repeated line | Simple melodic cell, then variation on a later return. | Is it recognizable when repeated? |
| Consequential word | Longer note, beat accent, or cleared space. | Does emphasis land on that word? |
| Name or unfamiliar term | One foreground lead, crisp consonants. | Can it be transcribed? |
| Reversal | Change one musical axis at that exact line. | Does the turn register? |

A deliberately breathless or mysterious line may call for a different choice.

## Natural stress and musical accent

Speech stress and musical accent can reinforce or disrupt each other. Accents arise from beat, duration, pitch, attack, or surrounding silence. Studies examined [song-prosody alignment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3225926/) and [sung-word recognition](https://doi.org/10.18061/emr.v9i1.3729). Inspect *important* words rather than scoring every beat in Style. The cited word-recognition material was sung in English; apply its stress findings most directly to English lyrics and test other languages by their own prosody.

“Speech-shaped verse phrases with natural word stress” gives a governing behavior. “The repeated two-word line lands firmly on the beat” names a special case. A deliberate mismatch can make a phrase unstable: “the opening line enters early against a steady pulse, then its answer resolves on the beat.” Avoid blanket “syncopated vocals” if a later claim must sound plain and decisive.

**Test:** Listen without reading. If a key word sounds mis-stressed, revise the phrasing cue or clear its accompaniment. One awkward generation need not cause a rewrite of the finished lyric.

## Density, breath, and melodic space

Syllable density depends on available time. A rapid line can ride a moderate pulse; a brisk track can carry measured vocals. Specify the relation: “quick verse syllables over long held chords.” Put a breath or reply after the complete thought, not automatically at every printed line break.

A melisma gives multiple notes to one syllable. It may spotlight a feeling, but it delays the next word. In the tested material of a [sung-word recognition study](https://doi.org/10.18061/emr.v9i1.3729), syllabic settings were more intelligible than melismatic ones. This warrants testing ornate delivery on high-priority words, not banning vocal runs. State a crucial hook simply first; decorate a later repetition or a wordless tail.

**Prompt hypothesis:** “Dense verses use measured, syllabic delivery over sparse chords; instrumental answers wait until complete sentences.” **Test:** Are the words clear and the replies truly in gaps? **Revision:** If the lead races, slow *its phrasing* without changing the entire track's tempo.

## Intelligibility is a scene, not a singer command

A word may disappear because consonants blur, voices overlap, bright instruments mask it, or a long run stretches its syllable. An experiment found a modest decline in word recognition with multiple simultaneous singers versus one, particularly for some final consonants; a survey found musicians perceived diction and acoustic environment as important, but did not establish causal effect sizes. See [multi-voice experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27068550/) and [intelligibility survey](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25249987/).

For a first statement of a key word, try one centered lead, clear consonant starts and releases, little ornament, and an accompaniment gap. A repeated phrase may later gain doubles or harmony. If a take loses a name, reduce overlapping support or thin the instrument attacking at the same time. “Make all lyrics clear” does not identify the cause. Keep the written word and change the audible conditions around it.

## Narrative turn and emotional relation

An arrangement can reinforce or complicate a lyric. Ask what a line changes, then mark it with *one* event: drum dropout, bass shift, closer lead, bare motif, or contrasting instrumental answer.

**Prompt hypothesis:** “The repeated line first rides a warm steady pulse; after the revelation, it returns with bare voice and one low sustained note.” **Test:** Do unchanged words now feel different? **Revision:** If the second statement is merely louder, specify the subtraction and remove a global build cue. This is a composition inference to test with the user's song, not a formula for emotion.

## Compile and test

The private analysis can be detailed. The final Style field usually needs only one cue for dense text, one for crucial-word clarity, and one for the major turn. Example: “Precise, near-spoken lead carries information-heavy verses over sparse chords; the repeated phrase opens into longer notes, and instruments fall away for the final reversal.” Do not duplicate lyric lines in Style.

Listen without a lyric sheet, then read along to locate masked or mis-stressed syllables. For one flawed section, [Suno section replacement](https://help.suno.com/en/articles/3271873/) offers local repair. For recurring failure, change one cue and log it. Preserve the finished text unless the user reopens writing.
