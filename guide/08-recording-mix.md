# 08. Recording and mix image

## Describe a listener's experience, not a settings sheet

The Style paragraph can request a recording image, but it is not a mixer project. A number for compressor ratio, reverberation time, or equalizer cutoff would imply a directness of control that a text generation workflow has not established. Describe the *audible result* and the relationship between sound sources. “A full ensemble shares a broad image; articulate vocal attacks remain distinct within sustained harmonic layers and active low lines” gives the model a scene with hierarchy. “Professional, clean, huge, warm, polished, raw, vintage, pristine” gives incompatible or redundant impressions with no clear roles.

An audio engineer can build these effects using level, equalization, panning, dynamic control, reverberation, delays, saturation, source selection, and performance. The [Ableton Live audio-effect reference](https://www.ableton.com/en/manual/live-audio-effect-reference/) documents how filters affect frequency content, reverberation parameters affect reflections and tails, compression responds to level, and its Utility effect changes stereo width or sums to mono. Those mechanics support the vocabulary below. They do not establish that a particular Suno prompt will map to a particular internal signal path.

## Foreground, depth, and air

**Foreground** means the listener's attention lands on a source. It is influenced by relative level, transient definition, intelligibility, spectral space, and dryness. The loudest element is not always the clearest: a lead can feel close at moderate level if consonants stay legible and a busy chord bed avoids its active range. In Style language, name the source and its relation: “the sung tune remains perceptible within continuous ensemble motion through distinct attacks and balanced registers.” The written design should identify how important words retain their role when other material enters.

**Depth** is not a single wet/dry switch. Direct sound, early reflections, delayed repeats, and a diffuse tail can imply different spaces. Ableton's reference explains early reflections as the first room echoes and pre-delay as the interval before reflections; decay describes how long the tail falls away. For this guide, these are explanatory mechanisms. A Style prompt should usually specify perception: “small, short room around the drums” or “long diffuse reflections behind the upper notes.” Avoid giving the entire mix the same long tail when intelligibility and attack are the song's main needs. If the vocal is meant to feel remote, say whether it remains intelligible: “distant lead with clear consonants” is a deliberate intended balance requiring source hierarchy.

**Air** can mean spectral openness, sparse arrangement, low noise, or a spacious tail. It is ambiguous until paired with a cause. “Airy” might produce hiss, wide pads, or a breathy vocal, any of which may be wrong. Choose “space between short chord stabs,” “soft upper harmonics around the lead,” or “a long, quiet tail after the final phrase.” Conversely, “intimate” might mean dry proximity, low arrangement density, quiet vocal delivery, or small-room reflections. Identify the intended cue.

## Width, center, and translation

Stereo width is the perceived left-to-right spread. A single central lead, central kick, and focused bass can anchor an otherwise wide frame. Wider chord layers, answering motifs, and reflections can create contrast around that anchor. “Wide” can identify which layers occupy a broad image and whether that image remains stable or changes. A distributed ensemble, wide backdrop or focused lead are different choices. A stable broad image may be intentional; playback on a small speaker or in mono may alter perceived width. Ableton documents both stereo-width adjustment and mono summing in its Utility effect, including why low-frequency mono compatibility may matter for playback. That is a production principle, not evidence that a generated file uses a particular technique.

## Frequency balance and separation

“Bright” and “dark” are not complete mix briefs. Brightness could come from crisp percussion, sharp consonants, upper-note motifs, or a broad treble lift; the emotional effect of each differs. “Warm” could mean strong low mids, rounded attacks, restrained upper harmonics, gentle saturation, or sustained lower-register tones. State which source supplies it. In the Ableton reference, low-pass and high-pass filters respectively attenuate content above or below a cutoff. That documents one engineering route to timbral difference, not a reason to put filter settings in the Style field.

Arrange spectral roles before asking for more equalization. A sustained bass note occupies low space longer than a short bass pluck. A dense chord bed may obscure the vocal even when each layer sounds good in isolation. An instrumental figure can answer in gaps or run simultaneously with the lead; distinct articulation, timing or register can clarify the intended relation, while deliberate blending is another option. A reverberant vocal can remain intelligible with suitable text timing and balance. This describes an intended allocation, not a rendered result.

Low-end descriptions should distinguish **weight** from **note definition**. A long sub tone can provide physical weight but may obscure bass pitch on small speakers; a shorter bass note with audible harmonics can make the line easier to follow. “Heavy bass” says little about the rhythmic role. A moving sustained bass can carry harmonic direction independently of shorter kick attacks; another design can coordinate their onsets. Specify the selected relation rather than defaulting to downbeat locks and gap replies. If bass and kick strike together, one can provide a sharp start and the other a sustaining body. If they alternate, the groove can feel active without extra percussion. These are arrangement relations that affect the recording image.

## Density is a time pattern

Layer count alone does not determine perceived density. Consider how long sounds should persist and how their attacks should overlap. Four short elements that take turns may feel clear; two sustained sources in the same register may feel opaque. Ask whether a layer should be present continuously, only on selected beats, between vocal lines, at transitions, or solely at the refrain. Specify the time pattern rather than using density as a mood instruction: “continuous chord and bass movement supports a long sung arc; short percussion attacks retain definition while the ensemble width stays stable across the return.” A lean texture is equally available when selected.

## Saturation, distortion, and surface texture

Saturation can add harmonics, thickness, and edge; obvious distortion can change the apparent source identity and reduce note clarity. The Ableton effect reference documents increasing drive as increasing distortion or saturation in several devices. A text prompt should identify where roughness belongs: “slight grain on the lead at peaks,” “mild harmonic edge on the bass,” or “fuzzy chord layer behind a clean vocal.” Asking for the entire mix to be both pristine and heavily distorted makes its hierarchy unclear. A clean foreground with textured backing can be coherent; a raw performance in a carefully separated mix can also be coherent if the axes are named separately.

## Dynamics without a hardware fantasy

Dynamic range is the contrast between quieter and louder moments, at the scale of an attack, phrase, section, or whole song. Compression can reduce peaks and alter apparent loudness; attack and release determine how it reacts over time. Ableton's compressor documentation explains these controls and the way fast settings can change transient character. For a Style prompt, translate the desired experience: “firm attacks with breathing room between hits,” “continuous full-ensemble impact with phrase-level vocal dynamic variation,” or “tight, even percussion under a more variable vocal.”

## Effects can be active timed material

A chosen source's delayed or reverberant continuation may contribute rhythm or harmony rather than merely decorate a word gap. State whether the tail overlaps the voice, marks a later pulse, carries a harmonic residue or answers a completed phrase. It needs a role just as a played part does. This is an original implementation of a possibility in the optional catalog, not a studio finding or a claimed processor setting.

Check what happens on chord changes and cuts. Continuing old chord tones can intentionally rub against new harmony, release before it, or be shortened; those are different designs. Total silence stops tails, whereas accompaniment rests may leave them sounding. A dense sustained image can be the intended texture, just as sharp separation can. Do not automatically shorten every source or make every return wider.

## Written image audit

Allocate foreground or shared attention, width, depth, roughness and persistence to the relevant sources and moments. Remove global contradictions; preserve intentional blend. Directness is independent of force and note length. A sustained dry source can remain near; a clear attack can have a quieter long tail behind it. None of these written choices establishes an internal signal path or a rendered result. No playback or measurements are required.

## Compile a coherent image

Choose a coherent image with the purposeful contrasts this song needs; no contrast count or spatial change is required. Describe the selected recording organization: foreground/background hierarchy, distributed ensemble attention, deliberate blend or articulated separation. There is no universal drafting order or requirement to keep the vocal dry while backing expands.

Before compiling, ask: Which source is in front? Which layer supplies width or distance? Which attacks need to stay distinct? Does any actual refrain need a spatial change, or should the image stay stable? Which crucial lyric line is most at risk of masking? Answer with source and section names. “Close lead” is incomplete if every competing layer is also pushed forward.
