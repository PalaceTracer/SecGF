# 04 — Harmony, melody, and hooks

## The decision this chapter supports

Specify what changes in pitch over time and what stays put. Harmony is the simultaneous support around a melody; melody is a timed path of pitches and rests. A prompt is more useful when it says whether chords hold or turn, whether the lead line climbs or circles, and which sound carries a recurring idea. “Beautiful melody and rich chords” predicts almost nothing. A developing sung phrase over traveling harmony specifies a different organization from a returning tune over stable chords; choose the relationship rather than treating either as the safe starting point.

Finished lyrics are the input. These definitions are compositional tools; Style phrasings are untested proposals, not exact note commands.

## Harmonic center and emotional color

A **tonal center** is the pitch that feels like rest. Preserve a supplied key when it matters; do not invent one. “Dark” and “bright” could describe harmony, register, timbre, or mix, so name the mechanism: “low repeating chords with a brighter upper voicing at the refrain.”

**Chord-set size** is the number of distinct harmonies in a loop. Chord vocabulary and harmonic development are choices with equal need for musical justification. A small cycle can stay stable, while a larger harmonic route can carry related phrases through changing destinations; neither is the default for narrative lyrics. [Berklee’s songwriting discussion](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/harmonic-progressions-for-songwriters/) explains how even one changed chord can create section contrast.

## Harmonic rhythm: rate of chord change

**Harmonic rhythm** is the rate of chord change, independent of tempo. Holding chords gives the lead or bass room to move; changing them more often can heighten anticipation. “Chords hold across long vocal phrases” differs from short repeated stabs on an unchanged chord. [Berklee’s composition article](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/music-composition-techniques-and-resources/) treats chord duration as a distinct compositional choice.

A **pedal tone** persists while harmony above it moves. A **suspension** holds a note across a chord change before it settles. A **departure and return** leaves the established loop and restores it later. Use one when the song needs that effect: “bridge briefly leaves the loop; final refrain returns.”

## Voicing, register, and attack

**Voicing** is the spacing and register of chord tones. A wide upper chord above a lean low foundation differs from a compact midrange block. State whether chords are held, pulsed, arpeggiated, or struck and damped; the attack pattern can change perceived motion without changing harmony.

An **arpeggio** sounds chord tones in sequence; a **stab** is a short chord attack; a **wash** sustains and blends. Voice leading is the path each tone takes between chords. “Small upper-note motion across held chords” is an audible description of smooth voice leading. See [Berklee’s technical explanation](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/voice-leading-paradigms-for-harmony-in-music-composition/).

## Melody as contour plus rhythm

**Melodic contour** is a line’s directional shape: rising, falling, hovering, or leaping then resolving. Rhythm, note length, register, and rests complete the design. [Berklee’s melody guide](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/conjunct-disjunct-melody-basic-definitions/) defines pitch and rhythm as core components. A long arch, an angular recurring figure or developing shorter cells combine contour and duration differently; singing does not privilege a narrow verse or a higher refrain.

**Register** is the pitch region; **range** is the span between low and high notes. A narrow span or a broad span can define a verse. Place important melodic destinations where the chosen phrasing needs them; the highest or longest note need not be a late climax.

## Motifs and the hook’s carrier

A **motif** is a small recognizable rhythmic or pitch figure that returns or transforms. It may belong to the voice, bass, chords, or a short lead sound. A motif may return literally or become recognizable through transformed rhythm, contour or carrier; choose the degree of recurrence this song needs. [Berklee’s motif guide](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/simple-tools-for-better-melodies/) offers this as a compositional method, not a promise of memorability.

A **hook** is a recognizable feature. If the chosen design uses one, specify the aspects of its carrier, shape, placement or recurrence that define it. Not every song needs a hook or a new refrain. “Catchy” describes a hoped-for result rather than its implementation.

**Call and response** alternates a phrase with an answering source. A **countermelody** runs simultaneously; register, articulation, duration, dynamics or staggered peaks can distinguish it when separation is intended. For dense lyrics, compare a phrase-gap reply with simultaneous independent accompaniment distinguished by articulation, register or phrase peaks. Neither is safer by default; choose the intended musical interaction.

## Section contrast without arbitrary complexity

### Independent melodic identities and phrase-led entry

A vocal tune and instrumental motif can move independently, double one another or exchange foreground. Decide whether an instrumental figure remains continuous, changes or recedes according to the selected organization; shared attention can be intentional.

Alternatively, let a meaningful finished phrase determine the first melodic entry: expose its tune early, then confirm the pulse with accompaniment. This does not require an unaccompanied opening or the insertion of new text. Phrase-first entry, instrumental prefiguration and delayed hook arrival are different choices. Phrase-led writing and independent instrumental/vocal construction are available design proposals here, not a tune to copy.

### Reserve, expand or suspend the melodic destination

Separate **pitch span**, **register**, **duration** and **projected force**. A refrain can widen intervals without getting louder, or hold a stressed vowel longer without climbing. A single late upper note can be reserved for an earned lyric destination; a continuously high tune has a different arc. Do not impose a narrow three-note verse on every song merely to guarantee singing. Specify definite pitch behavior with the range and motion this brief needs.

Choose a cyclic, traveling, cadence-led or pedal-centered harmonic organization before deciding its local changes. A new chord, different duration, bass path or voicing is one way to develop it; small edits do not have priority over a different harmonic route. Do not invent modulations to imply sophistication. If a source's chord changes need clarity, reduce a competing continuous wash or assign sharper chord attacks to one layer; atmospheric motion can remain elsewhere. A clear harmonic identity and a complex surrounding texture are separate variables.

Melodic destinations can recur, vary, resolve, remain suspended or develop across several phrases. Decide how they relate to actual text and harmony; a final note, higher refrain or withdrawal is not compulsory.

A contrast can retain a melodic, rhythmic or harmonic anchor while changing voicing, phrase timing, contour or chord rhythm in either direction. A stable relationship can also be intentional. [Berklee’s hook discussion](https://online.berklee.edu/takenote/how-to-write-songs-with-killer-hooks/) treats placement and contrast as useful songwriting choices. If recognition depends on a returning figure, identify it and how it returns.

## Harmonic sentences and several timescales

A harmonic route can be cyclic, pedal-centered, cadence-led or traveling. A cadence is a phrase-ending harmonic/melodic event with a chosen degree of closure. It can organize an unequal phrase span rather than arrive at identical production-block lengths. Do not equate every root-position bass note with tonal home, or every higher note with a key change. Stable harmony can focus attention on changing words; moving harmony can support a stable instrumental rhythm.

Voicing, inversion and harmonic rhythm are separate. Inversion changes the lowest chord tone without changing the chord root; voice leading describes how individual tones move. A compatible common upper tone can connect lower changes. At a conflicting change, release it, adapt it or carry continuity through rhythm rather than forcing one pitch. No key or progression is copied from a reference merely to make a prompt look precise.

Harmony need not be a continuous chord bed. Bass and melody can define intervals that imply or withhold harmonic grounding. A drone holds a center while upper notes change its tension; it is not the same as cycling a chord set. A repeated accompaniment figure can retain attack rhythm while its notes take different harmonic routes. The current brief decides whether harmonic simplicity, richness, ambiguity or directed closure is useful.

## Compose the pitch relationship

A chord cycle, pedal, changing bass route, sequence of cadences or through-developing harmony can organize a song. Decide whether harmonic rhythm follows the vocal phrase, crosses it or stays independent. Choose voicing and movement to support the selected sound, without translating each image or statement into a chord explanation.

A verse needs a positive sung melodic behavior when singing is intended. Choose a direction, interval gesture, length of phrase or rhythmic relationship to the pulse; the recognizable idea can return with different endings or move through changing harmony. Instrumental parts may double that melody, answer it, carry a separate motif or remain harmonically supportive. No one relationship is necessary for every verse.

## Phrase organization and motif transformation

A recurring pitched cell can be extended, contracted, sequenced or moved to a new harmonic setting. A longer phrase may cross several bars; several short cells may form one sentence. State the chosen relation rather than equating every line break with a bar or requiring all phrases to have equal length. In intended sung verses retain a recurring definite melodic identity at phrase or section scale as the melody develops through the text.

Literal repetition preserves pitches and rhythm; transposition preserves their interval pattern in another pitch region; freer variation can retain only a rhythm, opening gesture or destination. Specify the recognition needed. A held refrain note, a transformed verse cell, a low repeated interval or a cadence can have different responsibilities. No universal requirement for a single exposed hook, new chord, highest note or larger final return.

## Keep melodic ownership explicit

The vocal line, bass and upper figure can carry different musical identities at the same time. A bass melody may move through chord changes while the singer repeats a recognizable pitched phrase; an upper line may double selected notes, sustain a contrasting tone or play its own rhythm. Decide whether they overlap, exchange roles or move together for the chosen song.

A tune described only as an instrumental figure leaves the sung melody unspecified. For intended singing, add the voice's own contour, interval behavior or rhythmic phrase relation. Treat examples as composition choices: no fixed contour, chord count or final resolution is a universal default.

## Independent lines are more than answers

An **answer** follows a phrase. A **double** reinforces its contour simultaneously. **Counterpoint** coordinates independent melodic lines; an independent countermelody may run at the same time as the sung tune. **Imitation** introduces a recognizable cell later in another part. These relationships need not produce the same foreground hierarchy.

When words need priority, different register, note duration, attack, dynamics or staggered phrase peaks can help distinguish independent parts. Gap-only answers are one option, not a prohibition of simultaneous melody. Equal lines can intentionally share attention; a tune can lie inside an ensemble rather than always at its top. Do not request independent counterpoint and exact unison from the same pair at the same moment.

Original fragments: “The sustained sung arch overlaps a clipped rising instrumental line, with their peaks separated”; “A second pitched part takes up the cell after its first entrance while the earlier line continues”; “Bass and voice define the harmonic path through their moving intervals.” These are design proposals, not claims of a tradition's complete grammar or guaranteed generator behavior. If a chosen relation needs more explanation, research it proportionately; no omitted specialist file is required.

## Develop harmony and melody separately

A vocal melody can be supported by a chord cycle, a moving bass, a held tone with changing upper voices or a less settled harmonic path. Select how frequently harmony turns and whether voice and bass move together, independently or toward a shared destination. A passing chord is one option; harmonic development does not need to stop at one decorative color or a fixed two-bar pattern.

An instrumental figure may double the tune or provide a separate idea. Neither its register nor its contour specifies what the singer actually sings. When the verse is sung, include a concrete pitched vocal behavior and its relationship to the chosen accompaniment. An actual repeated passage can retain or vary its pitch shape and harmonic support according to the current brief.

## Develop pitch over more than one timescale

Choose whether harmony turns at phrase boundaries, moves underneath a continuing phrase, sustains a pedal while inner voices change, or follows another purposeful route. A recurring vocal idea can return at a different pitch, start on a different beat, continue across a line break, or change its ending while remaining recognizable. An instrumental line may double it, answer it, or pursue an independent contour simultaneously. These are alternatives to compare, not a progression or a required hook; choose the relationship that belongs to the selected song.

## Diagnostic questions

1. Is a tonal center supplied as a written fact, chosen as a proposal, or intentionally left unspecified?
2. Does the song’s emotional change require a new chord, a changed chord duration, a wider voicing, or a different melody?
3. Does harmonic vocabulary remain stable or change, and how do voicing, bass movement and harmonic rhythm contribute?
4. How are melodic destinations distributed across phrases: concentrated peak, several local arrivals or sustained continuity? Which text stresses shape them?
5. If the design has a hook, which part carries it and where does it appear or recur?
6. What does each simultaneous line contribute, and how do articulation, phrase timing, register or intentional blend allocate attention?
7. What feature remains recognizable across sections?
