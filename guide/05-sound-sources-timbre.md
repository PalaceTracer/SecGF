# 05 — Sound sources and timbre

## Choose roles before names

A Style field cannot carry an inventory of every sound. Decide what each needed source **does**: marks the pulse, carries bass, supports chords, states the hook, answers the lead, makes atmosphere, or punctuates a transition. Decide whether a role belongs to one carrier, a deliberately doubled ensemble, or several independent parts. Shared responsibility can define the design; clarify how it works. “Plucked upper figure answers the vocal” is often more useful than a list of possible instruments because it specifies register, articulation, and timing. A named instrument can still be useful when its characteristic sound is indispensable, but do not expect the name alone to determine performance.

Separate **source identity** from **timbre** and **part behavior**. A keyboard source may have a rounded, sustained tone or a bright, short one; the same nominal source can occupy foreground or background. A guitar-like source can strum chords, play short single notes, sustain a line, scrape rhythmically, or create noise. The Style prompt should carry the smallest combination that prevents an unwanted substitution: source family, audible tone, role, and timing. A sound that has no clear job in the listener’s mental image probably does not deserve prompt space.

Written reference prompts offer possible relationships, not a required palette. A useful transfer might be an acoustic attack against an electronic sustain, a short hook answered by a longer tone, or a melody passed between sources at a lyric return. Select the relationship because it serves this song. Exact instruments, tunings, model names, frequency bands, and processing settings in a reference are conditional choices. A liked prompt does not establish which of its requests appeared in the recording or which detail the user enjoyed.

## The four audible parts of a note

**Attack** is the start of a sound: abrupt, soft, picked, struck, breathy, bowed, or swelling. **Decay** is what happens after the initial peak. **Sustain** is the level and tone held while the note continues. **Release** is the tail after the note ends. This envelope distinguishes a clicky pluck from a slowly swelling pad even at the same pitch. The [Live 12 manual](https://www.ableton.com/en/live-manual/12/live-instrument-reference/) defines these stages operationally. A [controlled timbre study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5698532/) manipulated attack and release information during source identification, showing that temporal envelope is one contributor to recognizing a sound; it does not reduce all source identity to the envelope alone.

For Style, describe the listener’s experience instead of numbers: “quick attack and short decay,” “rounded onset with a long, airy tail,” “soft swell that blooms after the lead phrase.” A transient can supply rhythm even when the note is harmonically simple. A sustained sound can provide continuity without frequent attacks. The selected envelope should fit the lyric: short plucks may leave consonants exposed; long tails may support held vowels but crowd fast sung syllables if placed in the same range.

**Articulation** concerns how one note connects to the next. Detached notes leave gaps; connected notes form a line. Muted attacks emphasize rhythm over ring. A tremoring or repeated articulation adds activity without changing pitch. Do not infer articulation from source name. “Muted, sharply cut chord strokes” and “slowly connected chord tones” are distinct even if both use the same instrument family.

## Brightness, spectrum, and noise

**Brightness** often refers to stronger upper-frequency content relative to lower content, but it also depends on attack and register. A bright onset with a warm tail differs from a continuously thin tone. **Dark** may mean softened upper partials, a low register, or a recessed mix position; specify which is intended. **Hollow**, **nasal**, **metallic**, **glassy**, **woody**, and **grainy** are useful only when paired with role and envelope. “A short metallic two-note answer” gives more direction than “metallic atmosphere.”

A filter can attenuate parts of a spectrum; a moving filter can make a held sound change color over time. A noise component can add hiss, breath, scrape, or roughness. Modulation can vary pitch, amplitude, or tone periodically or irregularly. The [Live 12 manual](https://www.ableton.com/en/live-manual/12/live-instrument-reference/) describes these mechanisms. In Style, describe the audible motion: “a low sustained tone slowly opens brighter,” “a narrow high note wavers gently,” or “a dry strike carries a brief noise edge.” Avoid device parameters unless a user specifically asks for production settings. Text generation may imitate the result without implementing the mechanism literally.

**Warm**, **thick**, **shimmering**, and **vintage** are underspecified by themselves. Translate them. Warm might mean soft attacks and low-mid weight, or gentle saturation, or muted upper content; choose the intended cause. Thick might mean a doubled line, close chord spacing, heavy bass, or long overlapping releases. Shimmering might mean bright sustained high partials with slow movement. Do not force every sound to share one adjective. Contrast gives hierarchy: a dry attack can sit in front of a long diffuse layer, and a dark bass can support a bright short hook.

## Translate processing into a perceptible job

Choose a target source, an audible change, and a location before naming a treatment. A device list spends characters without establishing what remains clear or what changes. The following are original prompt hypotheses, not promises that Suno will instantiate a particular processor.

| Reference shorthand | Operational choice | Compact candidate wording |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Named electric-piano or organ model | Short rounded chord attack versus a hollow sustained chord; foreground versus backing | “Rounded keyboard stabs answer the lead endings.” |
| Bitcrushed texture | A deliberately coarse digital edge on one fill or hook layer | “A coarse digital pattern continues beneath sustained singing, with clipped attacks distinct from the vocal arc.” |
| Saturated texture | A roughened body or compressed attack on a selected source | “The guitar gains a gritty sustained edge on the refrain while the lead stays clear.” |
| Low-pass automation | A specific source becomes duller or brighter across a transition | “The held synth chord gradually opens brighter before the return.” |
| Slap or rhythmic delay | A short echo thickens an attack, or distinct repeats answer a gap | “One dark echo answers the final sung word after the lead releases.” |
| Named plate or hall | Allocate decay and articulation to the selected voice/instrument relationship | “A sustained reverberant lead floats above sharply articulated ensemble chords; word onsets remain distinct.” |

Keep a technique label when it concisely names an essential texture; pair it with its source and role. Exact delay fractions or settings belong only when the current brief makes that rhythm decisive. Distortion across the whole recording, simultaneous bright fills under dense sung words, and long echoes on every line are separate design decisions; they do not follow automatically from choosing one processed source. Before a requested complete silence, decide whether effect tails also stop. A cut in note attacks with echoes still ringing is a different event.

## Acoustic, electric, sampled, and synthesized behavior

These source families describe production possibilities, not fixed emotional properties. A physically excited source may show tiny variation from one strike to another; a sample may repeat exactly or be deliberately varied; a synthesized tone can be static or highly modulated. A text prompt should describe what is audible: “slight variation in repeated upper attacks,” “identical crisp low hits,” “sustained tone with slow brightness movement.” It should not use “organic” as a substitute for timing, tone, and dynamics.

For acoustic/electronic coexistence, decide whether sources share, double, alternate or independently carry rhythmic, harmonic and melodic responsibilities. Describe the relation and timbral interaction that matters to the current song. Do not make both families compete for every chord, attack, and lead answer simply to demonstrate a hybrid. Dense sung words may coexist with continuous independent accompaniment through suitable articulation, register and staggered peaks. Use gaps when alternation is the chosen character, not as an automatic remedy.

## Performed band character when requested

When the brief calls for a naturally performed band sound, specify performance roles: distinct electric rhythm and lead guitar articulations, bass movement that supports or moves independently of the kick, and acoustic drum-kit attacks with phrase-responsive cymbal and snare dynamics. Let the lead carry a pitched melody with natural phrase shaping; decide whether backing voices are selective or continuous. Choose the polish and room character for the song. These are selectable design moves, not a default arrangement or a guarantee of musicians or generated sound.

Synths, pads and doubled parts are intentional timbral choices. If an electronic layer is wanted, assign it a clear role and level; if the user wants a guitar-led band without it, name desired band sources positively and put necessary avoidance in EXCLUDE. Do not assume that a synthesizer caused a particular result or that melodic rock must omit electronics.

## Turn source choices into an arrangement

Name a source family when its sound is important, then give it a role. A repeating electronic figure might carry the subdivision while a separate bass line changes pitch; an amplified ensemble might distribute rhythm and melody across independent parts; acoustic sources might contribute pulse, sustained harmony or a melodic counterline. These are selectable partial relationships, not genre recipes or preferred instrument lists.

Choose how attacks and sustain interact. A source can stay present throughout, hand off a motif, double it, change its envelope or leave an intentional space. The number of sources and degree of processing are decisions for the selected sound. If word clarity matters, coordinate register, articulation and balance; do not infer that the arrangement must have only one instrument.

## Roles and interaction map

| Role | Candidate audible design | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rhythm bed | Repeated clipped attacks on a stable subdivision | Duplicating hats or competing with consonants |
| Harmonic support | Held, widely spaced tones behind the lead | Tails blurring chord changes or lyrics |
| Hook carrier | Short recognizable figure in one exposed register | Hook repeating so often it loses emphasis |
| Counterline | An independent simultaneous melodic path | Unplanned competing peaks or indistinguishable roles |
| Reply | A figure after a completed phrase | Insufficient space before the next entry |
| Atmosphere | Low-level evolving noise or sustained color | Texture becoming louder than the song’s core |
| Transition marker | One brief swell, strike, or drop | Constant decoration erasing section boundaries |

## Density, register, and layers

**Layer count** is distinct from perceptual density. Two long overlapping sounds can fill more space than five short punctuations. Density rises with overlapping durations, similar registers, repeated attacks, and reverb tails. Choose purposeful overlap, separation or blend through rhythm, register, articulation and relative level. Reduction can address a particular masking conflict; it is not the definition of vocal clarity. A full ensemble can sustain momentum with coordinated attacks or independent lines under pitched singing. The songwriter has only written inputs, so these remain intended relationships.

**Register handoff** and simultaneous layering create different textures; neither is intrinsically preferable. A high figure can disappear when the lead moves high, then return when the voice descends. A low pad can recede when bass becomes active. “The high pluck answers only in vocal gaps” encodes this as an audible arrangement rule. If the prompt budget is tight, one such interaction outranks a long list of source names.

## Coordinate active sources

Choose source roles in relation to each other. A rhythmic layer may use short attacks while a harmonic layer sustains; an independent bass line can move below a vocal contour; a counterline can cross the vocal phrase rather than wait for its end. A full band or layered electronic texture can retain continuous movement while the voice stays intelligible through distinct timing, register or balance.

Describe the identity and job of consequential sources, including distortion, envelope or articulation when these define the sound. Doubling, sustained layers and foreground exchange are available choices; none needs to occur only in a closing phrase. If a simple arrangement is chosen, specify what makes its few parts musically distinctive rather than adding unused instruments.

## Hook carrier, handoff, and doubling

Source hierarchy can change as well as source identity. A bass tone may carry the repeating identity rather than act only as low support; specify its articulation and pitch role, then let drums support or answer it. Do not infer kick alignment from bass distortion. A piano and another melodic source may briefly share foreground during an instrumental passage, then yield to one sung lead. This local equality does not mean every part should be equally prominent throughout.

Likewise, a percussion figure can remain the continuity cue while guitars, electronics or sustained chords change around it. Describe the recurring attack behavior before its exact source if the pattern is the point. A recording credit establishes participation only; it cannot establish source timing, envelope, foreground position or hook ownership. The anonymous production research supplies some documented role decisions and many unresolved ones; new allocations must remain proposals fitted to the finished lyric.

Distinguish three arrangements. A **reply** adds a figure after a lead phrase. A **handoff** passes an established melody to another source; the first carrier may rest or overlap briefly during the transfer. A **double** plays the same melodic contour together with the carrier. Identify the motif by contour or rhythm so recognition survives a timbre change. These options are selectable; the new song does not need all three.

| Choice | Original design move | What stays recognizable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reply | “A bowed two-note answer waits until each verse line ends.” | Answer contour and gap placement. |
| Handoff | “The opening plucked figure returns on a soft synth at the lyric's repeated line.” | Figure's rhythm and pitch shape; source changes. |
| Double | “A restrained guitar doubles the held refrain melody, then rests for the next verse.” | Sung hook remains foreground; reinforcement is local. |
| Envelope development | “The same clipped keyboard motif lengthens into ringing notes on the return.” | Carrier and motif remain; note duration changes. |

A wordless processed vocal can occupy a hook or answer role when the chosen direction supports it; keep it distinct from the intelligible sung lead. Source handoff is especially useful when another layer would overload the lead register. Doubling may supply emphasis, but its first statement can remain exposed. State the intended foreground, shared attention or blend when material. One source yielding space is an option; coequal lines and continuous doubling can also be deliberate.

## Turn source selection into interaction

For each important source, decide whether it supplies rhythmic drive, a harmonic bed, a recognizable melodic thread, a second independent line, or a changing texture. The same source may change jobs, and multiple sources may share a job by doubling or blending. To describe a fuller image, give overlapping parts different attacks, registers or durations where that helps distinguish them; coordinated simultaneous attacks can instead supply collective weight. A sustained distorted electronic layer can move internally while percussion and voice continue; acoustic and electronic parts can also coexist without one becoming decoration. These are selectable behaviors, not a gear checklist.

## Give a fuller arrangement distinct responsibilities

A full texture can keep several parts active while giving them different rhythms, pitch ranges, note lengths or functions. A rhythm source can maintain motion, a bass can shape the harmony, and another part can carry sustained chords or an independent motif under singing. The number and prominence of parts are choices for the sound, not a test of how many syllables the lyric contains.

Choose audible envelopes and colors: a short articulated attack can coexist with a sustained or distorted layer; a melodic source can continue under the voice or answer it; a doubled part can create weight. This describes possible relationships, not a requirement to add layers. An intentionally sparse arrangement remains a valid direction when requested or justified.

## Ensemble organization can be the primary identity

Source identity, part and performer count are different. A single fingerpicked instrument can maintain a bass pulse while its upper notes form an independent line; multiple sources can articulate one shared melody. A block ensemble can move in coordinated chords; a contrapuntal ensemble can keep several paths distinct. Neither is defined by a longer instrument list.

An ensemble may organize attention through one foreground, an inner tune with surrounding parts, deliberate equality, continuous shared melody, or alternating section statements. Choose the relationship, not a rule that all other parts must wait for a lead to stop. A rhythm section can stay continuous while arranged sections answer one another; a whole rhythm-section ownership change is different from adding guitar decoration over an unchanged programmed beat.

A small ensemble may state a theme, develop it in an actual instrumental space and return it, whereas a larger arranged ensemble can build its argument through coordinated sections. Neither requires invented lyric repetitions or a solo break. If the brief already supplies every sung line continuously, preserve that time and adapt instrumental responsibility around it.

## Accompaniment can carry an independent argument

A piano, plucked figure or sustained line can anticipate, question or continue a sung phrase through contour, harmonic tension or placement. It need not repeat the singer or merely fill gaps. Define what its activity contributes, then decide which moments need equality and which need vocal priority. The intended relationship can deliberately complicate the words without borrowing a source story.

New color can come from changing an existing part's envelope, register, roughness or role instead of always adding a part. Purposeful doubling can also be the point: shared attack or octave reinforcement is not automatically redundant. Continuous acoustic/electronic coexistence, alternating ownership and envelope contrast are separate useful choices. Reject a color that does not serve the current brief; no emotion has a mandatory timbre.
