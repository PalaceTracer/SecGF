# 07 — Section form and dynamic arc

## Build from the lyric's events

Form is the audible pattern of entrance, return, contrast, and ending. Read the *finished* lyric before designing it. Mark where new information appears, a phrase repeats, a perspective changes, and the last statement lands. The Style field should specify only the musical changes that make those events legible; the words and useful section headings stay in Lyrics. [Suno Custom Mode](https://help.suno.com/en/articles/2415873/) provides separate lyric input. Every section cue suggested here is a **listening hypothesis**, not a guaranteed instruction to the generator.

Do not impose a fixed sequence. Assign each actual section a function—information, anticipation, recognition, reconsideration, or resolution—even when the lyric has no repeated hook.

## Six dimensions of change

For each lyric section, make a private map. Which source has **focus**? What is the **pulse**? How many events sound at once (**density**)? Where do melody and bass sit (**register**)? Is the image near or wide (**space**)? What sound has been withheld or established that the listener can expect next? Choose the two dimensions that matter most. “Build” by itself does not identify a cause.

| Change | Possible audible event | Different from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Density | A harmony enters behind the lead. | Overall loudness. |
| Activity | More short percussion attacks occur. | Numerical tempo. |
| Register | The lead rises on a repeated word. | Added layers. |
| Width | Background parts spread while lead stays centered. | Vocal distance. |
| Rhythm weight | Fast subdivisions recede while a broad backbeat becomes prominent. | A slower tempo. |
| Silence | A one-beat stop exposes the next entrance. | Gradual thinning. |

Experiments link intensity profile with perceived arousal and temporal expectation with tension. Treat level and timing as separate levers, not an emotional formula: [intensity](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3080387/), [expectation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11297889/).

## Opening and information

Give the opening one recognizable cue: a short figure, bare vocal, incomplete pulse, sustained chord, or interruption. “Dry voice over a two-note pulse; bass enters with the repeated line” specifies more than “start sparse and build.” If the full arrangement begins immediately, develop through subtraction, distance, or rhythm.

A section carrying new facts needs room for the lead: fewer chord attacks, replies after complete lines, or a stable motif. A forceful beat can leave holes for consonants. Specify the cause: “single close lead over clipped chords and a steady low pulse.”

**Test:** On the first listen, does the opening cue remain in memory, and can the new words be followed? If the first verse is crowded, remove a competing role rather than adding another vague “clear vocal” instruction.

## Anticipation and arrival

An anticipation section changes one or two axes to make an arrival apparent. It can raise the lead, increase subdivisions, add backing voices, widen space, change chord duration, or remove a layer just before the return. Ask what the arrival offers that the prior section withholds. A held vocal refrain may follow clipped phrases. A low-end impact may follow a bar with little bass. A lyrical reversal may need a gap before its first word. The [metrical expectation experiment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11297889/) supports a connection between timing and tension; the exact musical effect remains a design hypothesis.

An arrival needs an identity: repeated words, melodic contour, instrumental hook, chord change, or a particular combination of sound sources. It need not be the loudest section. “Short verse cells become held lead notes over a broad half-time pattern” gives a contrast; “powerful chorus” does not. If the arrival repeats, keep enough of its identity for recognition. A final return can add a harmony, answer, or new bass movement without replacing the hook.

**Prompt hypothesis:** “The drums thin before the repeated line; the low end returns as the lead opens into two held notes.” **Test:** Can a listener locate the arrival without section tags? **Revision:** If it has no effect, strengthen the one most important subtraction or entrance. If it is crowded, remove a layer rather than adding a crescendo.

## Contrast, return, and ending

A contrasting section changes perspective while preserving a motif, pulse, harmonic center, or vocal contour. Expose one lead where voices stacked, halve chord attacks, or shift the hook to another source.

For the final return, ask whether the repeated lyric means something different after the intervening line. If it resolves, the voice may firm and support may widen. If it now sounds doubtful, percussion may fall away. A one-time final sentence can be most effective with the arrangement cleared around it. Form follows this song's meaning; the final passage need not always grow.

End with an action: dry stop, held chord after the lead, instrumental reply, fade, or opening texture returned. Name it in Style only if vital.

**Prompt hypothesis:** “The repeated line returns with its melody intact but only voice and low pulse; the last word ends dry before one instrumental answer.” **Test:** Does the reduction change the line's meaning and does the answer wait? **Revision:** If the take simply gets louder, remove global build language and state the subtraction.

## Resolve contradictions before compilation

“Sparse and full” needs a section boundary. “Driving beat throughout” and “total drum dropout” need an explicit exception. “Close lead” and “huge room” can coexist if the room belongs to backing layers. “Gradually louder” and “sudden stop” can coexist if the stop marks the climax. Make the relationship explicit rather than repeating conflicting adjectives.

Draft a section map outside the final field, then retain no more than three high-value relationships. Original compact candidates:

- “Bare lead and muted two-note figure open; bass arrives at the first repeated line, and a narrow upper harmony enters only on the final return.”
- “Dense verse phrases meet a one-beat stop; the refrain lands on sustained words over a broad half-time pulse.”
- “The second return strips to voice and low pulse; the last return restores the full arrangement.”

These are components to adapt, not stock templates. If the lyric lacks a repeated section, do not invent one to use a preferred phrase.

## Listening and revision

Before generation, answer: What is the opening cue? What is withheld? Which section carries new information? Where is the first unambiguous arrival? What changes on the final return? How does the last sound terminate? Select only answers that affect the recording.

Listen once without the lyric sheet and mark actual boundaries. Is each important entrance audible, and does a return sound related to its first statement? Read along on a second pass to check alignment with the words. Record observed, absent, and ambiguous cues. If all sections are equally dense, bind one layer to a later section. If a lift fails, name the exact entrance or silence. If a swell lands on the wrong line, identify the intended section and remove a conflicting global cue. Change one cause at a time and record model and settings. For a strong take with one flawed transition, [Suno's Song Editor](https://help.suno.com/en/articles/6141505/) documents local section editing; its availability does not prove any Style phrase will be obeyed.
