About Music Production
Music is produced in a couple of stages. Generally we may say there are the following stages:
Music composition - you compose your music with building blocks of music. You learn these blocks by learning music theory.
Instrumentation - you substitute your composed notes with various instruments, or in other words, you make some instruments play some specific notes. Vocal sings one note, bass plays the other note, and drums provide the rhythm.
Mixing - you use various tools to clean the sound and make it sound coherent. Then you render the file.
After this, your music is ready for distribution. This book focuses on the first stage: composition. First, we will learn about music theory and building blocks of music. Then we will learn how to put these blocks together into short or long songs.
(Where does recording live instruments fit in all of this? On the 2nd stage - you can record your instruments in a home studio, or produce printed notes for musicians to play and record in a more professional setting.)
Instrumentation and mixing is not discussed in this book, although I do discuss some elements of music production such as DAW, virtual instruments and even some hardware, because these are essentials. More on that later.
Some of you may already know how to play an instrument and just want to figure out how to use their knowledge in reference to MIDI software. Others have no musical experience whatsoever. This second type of reader, at the very beginning of musical journey, may wonder: how do I create this particular sound or that particular sound. Well, use specific instruments playing specific notes composed in accordance to specific rules governing a given genre, and when all these things blend together nicely, you get this particular sound.
It sounds abstract, I know - but hopefully at the end of this book you will have a much clearer picture.
This book is an introduction to music theory from the perspective of a computer musician, working not with classic notation, but with MIDI grid and DAW computer software. The first part of this book explains the basics of music theory:

This knowledge is required if you want to compose your own music, no matter the genre. If you want to make EDM, orchestral soundtracks for games or accompaniment for your own vocals, learning music theory is the first step.

Virtual Instruments
To make music in a DAW program, you also need virtual instruments. Let’s learn some basic stuff about them and learn how to use them in general. Actual music made on a computer is possible thanks to virtual instruments - virtual guitars, virtual drums, virtual flutes, virtual orchestras and more. These instruments come in various forms:
As stand-alone VSTi instruments that you can download and install, either via a wizard, or manually by copying it to a proper folder on your computer.
As sampled libraries that require a sampler to run.
Stand-Alone Instruments
These are basically small computer programs that run on their own and they don’t need any other piece of software except a DAW to run. These can be various instruments, like guitars or drums, but the most popular type of this kind of software are synths that are often used for electronic music, ambient, sound design, but have found their way into many different genres.
Sampled Instruments powered by a Sampler
The second type of virtual instruments are special “packages” that require a sampler to run.
A sampler is a computer program that can be run “stand alone” (individually), or as a middleware, inside your Digital Audio Workstation. There are many samplers on the market - a few examples are Kontakt by Native Instruments, Play 6 by EastWest/Quantum Leap, or Play Engine by Best Services.
These samplers are players. Like an audio player, or media player, or video player - but instead of simple sounds of videos, they allow you to play various instruments. These instruments - “sample libraries”, or simply virtual instruments - are created by developers (programmers, musicians) to make them as realistic as possible.
Music made with virtual instruments can be found anywhere these days - EDM, pop, but also film or game soundtracks.

Time Signature and BPM Controls
DAWs allow you to set the BPM value, that is: beats per minute, that define the tempo of the track; as well as time signature (this famous 4/4 thing that we will discuss later in greater details), as well as playback speed.
Figure 0.6 - Tempo (1) and meter (2) in Reaper. You can see that the meter is set to 4/4, and the tempo is set to 120.
Figure 0.7 - Meter and Tempo in Studio One.
BPM value is the number of beats per minute, this is what sets the tempo of the music. The faster the music, the greater the BPM value. For example, 70 BPM is slower than 140 BPM. The BPM and time signature settings here, in the control panel, are the main settings for the entire track. But most DAWs allow you to change both of these values multiple times throughout the track using special markers.
Most DAWs also allow you to change the rate of the playback, the actual speed. While tempo is a musical term and is defined by BPM, the speed is just telling you how fast the sound is being played - the faster the speed rate, the more you sound like a cartoon squirrel that had too much coffee.

One of the buttons mentioned earlier was used to control phasing issues. What is phasing ? Basically, phasing is a troubling situation in which two soundwaves cancel each other. This is a matter of physics. In simple terms, you can record two instruments on the separate tracks. When you listen to them individually, everything is fine - you can hear them loud and clear. But when you play them together, they sound quieter, or can’t be heard at all. This is because their sound waves cancel each other out either completely, or partially. You click the “phase” button to invert the sound waves so they won’t cancel each other out.

MIDI Editor
DAW programs usually have a MIDI editor, too.
Figure 0.19 - Midi editor in Reaper. Here you can see a piano roll on the left (3), a control cursor (2), and a note currently highlighted by a mouse cursor (1).

In Reaper, you can open the MIDI editor by double-clicking a MIDI item placed in a workspace.
MIDI editor is basically a grid with a “piano roll” on the left, which is what the name suggests - it’s a piano keyboard showing all the notes you can use in your track. The vertical axis on the grid represents the pitch of the sound/note (high or low), and the horizontal line represents the time and bars. I will tell you which key represents which note in just a moment.
It is the MIDI editor where we actually place notes, create melodies and harmonies for our music. You can add notes by using a mouse to click and drag the notes somewhere on the grid. Thus, even if you don’t own a MIDI keyboard, you can still create music in a more manual way.
All DAW programs also allow you to use MIDI devices such as the MIDI keyboard which you connect to your computer and use like a normal instrument. Using a mouse is easy - click, double click, or click and drag, depending on the DAW you’re using. As for using special MIDI keyboards, they’re often USB based. You just plug them to your computer, and configure it inside a DAW. There’s usually some kind of settings panel, where you can detect and configure a MIDI keyboard.
Figure 0.22 - In Reaper, go to Options -> Preferences, and then to MIDI Devices to configure your MIDI controller.
With a keyboard plugged in and detected, you can record yourself playing. If you’re a skilled piano player, this will be the most natural way to compose music. To record yourself playing, you need to “arm” the track - this means you need to select which track you want to record. Then, you hit the main “record” button in your DAW and start playing. The DAW will record your input data and you will actually record MIDI notes that you can edit in MIDI editor.
Figure 0.23 - The arrow shows the “arm” button pressed. This tells the DAW this is the track you want to record to.
If you own a MIDI keyboard, but you aren’t a skilled piano player, most DAWs support “step input”, and you can press piano keys on your keyboard one at the same, and your DAW will record MIDI notes step by step - thus the term, step input.

As figure 0.24 shows, in Reaper, in the MIDI editor, all you have to do is to select the button with a foot icon on it, and step input will be enabled.

Same Principles, Different Genres
Before we even get to learning about music theory, harmony and composition, I want to explain to you something, because once you start reading about theory, you may wonder how all of this applies to the genre you want to make.
Under each piece of music you ever heard we can find the same building blocks: notes and chords. How is it that the same things can sound as different genres? Basically, there are two things to consider:
Instruments used and their timbre.
The principles behind the genre.
Each instrument has a different timbre - it sounds in its own unique way. Because of the timbre we can tell violin and cello apart. And because of the timbre we can tell a French Horn and trance lead apart. The same chord, with all the same notes, can sound very differently based on what instruments we use. This is the first thing that makes the genre.
Principles behind the genre is the second thing. For example, jazz often uses dissonant chords and syncopation to achieve this unique, “jazzy” feeling. Dissonant chords and syncopation can be such a rule defining a genre. Orchestral music uses real players with their limitations: the simple fact that a brass player needs to breathe is a principle of the genre that says you can’t really have infinite trumpet solos. Another orchestral principle says that piccolo cannot physically play in the same register as the lowest register of the double bass.
Heavy metal music? Well: verse, chorus, verse, chorus, guitar solo, chorus and you’re done. And what about trance music? Add a beat, set the tempo to 138 and use an “Uplifting Lead” virtual instrument and you got yourself a club mix. Of course I’m simplifying here, using stereotypes, but basically this is very true.
With all these various instruments, their timbres, and rules and principles of different genres, we can create all sorts of music. And yet the core principles will remain the same - under each gene we have the same notes, same chords and chord progressions, same intervals, and so on.
Whenever you see a chord or a note in this book, know that it can be “orchestrated” or “instrumented” in so many different ways - the same chord can become a trance piece, or a jazz piece, or an orchestral piece.
Imagine the building blocks of music you’re going to learn very soon, such as notes, intervals or chords, as a scaffolding. The scaffolding can be always the same, but the materials you will use to build upon it (instruments, principles, timbres, textures, genres) will result in different kinds of music.
The notes are the same - what you do with them is what makes you a composer.
Now we can finally talk about music theory. I didn’t make audio files for some of this theoretical content (maybe most of it?), because I want to encourage you to type most of these examples yourself - this will familiarize you with working with DAW and it will also help you memorize what you read about.

The Notes
The basic element of music is a note.
Notes are sounds represented by specific symbols that we call notes. A note is a graphical representation of sound. As such, a note can describe both the pitch of the sound and its duration (how long it is).

The staff is a classic way to notate music and it is used by musicians to this day for a simple reason: it works very well. But if you make music on a computer, you don’t have to read classic notation (great news for hobbyists!) - you can create music simply by using a MIDI grid. MIDI Grid

Figure 1.6 shows a MIDI grid in a DAW program.

Thus, we have seven white notes and five black notes - twelve notes in total. All western music is made of only these twelve notes.
Here’s where things get tricky - notice that if you lower the note C a semitone down using a flat symbol, you will find yourself playing note B - a while key. There is no black key between notes B and C. And the piano roll picture shows that the chromatic notes on the left have two different names assigned. Why?
Note Cb (C flat) is, in reality, a note B - this situation, when a single note can have two different names, happens a few times on a piano roll and we say that these notes are enharmonic. So how do we know whether a note should be called Cb, or B? This depends on the context - what scale are we using and what chords are we using. Just know that if you have a scale, you can’t have the same two notes in a scale. If a scale contains A and A# notes, you need to change them to A and Bb, because you can’t have two A notes in the scale. If you’re a beginner, don’t worry about this too much, just remember that notes can be enharmonic and that this phenomenon simply happens.
For now just remember that in total, we have 12 chromatic notes. Try to count all the keys starting with note C and going up to note B. You will get 12 keys.
Now you know the notes. Let’s recall: we have C, D, E, F, G, A and B, as well as chromatic notes between them as sharps or flats. In total, these notes make the chromatic scale - the basis for western music.
The vertical axis on the MIDI grid represents the pitch of the note. The higher up the axis, the higher the sound.

By creating music, we simply work with these elements, playing all of them in a given rhythm.

2. Rhythm and meter
Now that you know notes, pauses and tempo, the final basic element of music theory is rhythm and meter. Rhythm is that constant “pulse” in music, this thing you tap your leg to. Meter is used to group various notes together into bars and groups of bars, so that we can later on use these groups to create a form for our music. In this context, tempo simply tells us how fast both the rhythm and meter are.
Let’s start with the rhythm. In itself, rhythm is a concept we all understand intuitively. It’s part of our human nature.
In case of music theory though, we discuss rhythm more in the context of how to notate it.
Rhythm is a way to vary the length of the notes in some organized way and to create some kind of notes pattern. In practice, rhythm is a number and/or form of accented fragments of the bar, and also the regularity or irregularity of the sound. To put it even simpler, rhythm is a pattern of pulsing sounds in a given time period.
The best way to understand this is by taking a look at some examples. Figure 2.1 shows the meter settings in Reaper, by default set to 4/4. In most DAWs, the default settings are 120 BPM for tempo and 4/4 for time signature (meter).

Syncopation
Syncopation, basically, is placing an accent on a different beat than normally, or in other words, changing the rhythm pattern for a short period of time.
Consider the following: let’s say that, in 4/4 meter, we normally place an accent on the first and third beat. Syncopation is meant to change, or break, this pattern, so we may place accent on the fourth beat instead of third one, or we may even get crazy and place and accent on a note that does not fall into a beat, like a note played between third and fourth beat - the rhythm pattern has been changed.
Syncopation allows us to change our rhythm’s pattern strategically - we play normal rhythm, then we change it with syncopation, and then we return to normal rhythm like nothing happened.
Or, you can simply move the accent left or right.

3. Intervals
An interval is the distance in pitch between two notes. This distance is the basis for building scales and chords. Intervals have different names and you need to learn these names and the distances they represent to be able to read other books on music and communicate with other people. For example, there is an interval called a perfect fifth, and it is equal to seven semitones apart. You may read in some book that you need to place two notes a perfect fifth apart, and thus, by knowing intervals, you know exactly where to put these notes.
A musical scale is made of specific intervals, and chords are, too, made of specific intervals. Later on, all of this will become clear. For now, let’s learn the intervals.

Every note is always a semitone apart from the next note - meaning that the distance between C and C# is a single semitone, C# to D is a single semitone and so on. This distance way of categorizing intervals will be discussed a bit later. First, let’s discuss the type of an interval:
Major interval contains two semitones between the notes. Seconds, thirds, sixths and sevenths can be major intervals.
Minor interval contains one semitone less than a major interval, or contains only a single semitone between the two notes. Seconds, thirds, sixths and sevenths can be minor intervals.
Only primes, octaves, fourths and fifths can be perfect intervals.
Diminished intervals contain one semitone less than a minor or perfect interval. Every interval, except a prime, can be diminished.
Augmented intervals contain one semitone more than a minor or perfect interval. Every interval can be augmented.
Quite simple, isn’t it? Not really? We don’t say “play a note four semitones above C”, but we say “play a major third above C”. And every book or course or video uses these words and phrases, so it’s a good idea to learn how to use the intervals.

Consonant and Dissonant Intervals
There is one more way to categorize intervals - as consonant and dissonant intervals. This way of categorizing refers to how these intervals sound - either pleasant, or unpleasant - good or chaotic.
What’s the difference? Consonant intervals sound good together, they are pleasant to the ear. On the other hand, dissonant intervals sound unpleasant, like there’s something disharmonious in them. By listening to the intervals from the files for this book, you must have heard this - some intervals were “nice”, and others were “bad”. This is the consonance and dissonance.
These two terms are used not only for the intervals but for all the sounds, chords or instruments, that either sound good together, bad together, or somewhere in between. We add both consonance and dissonance to our music, and this is a part of composition, when we want to achieve specific goals with our music, for example specific emotions. Each of the intervals plays its unique role in music.
Some sounds can be perceived as harmonious, nice, and pleasant. Other sounds can be perceived as chaotic, disharmonious, and unpleasant.

Intervals and Emotions
We often associate emotions with the intervals. It’s quite subjective, because each person can have different emotional associations with given chords or intervals, but some things are quite common between people, so most music theory books mention some “emotional keywords” here and there.
Basically, consonant intervals are associated with more positive emotions, while dissonant intervals are associated with negative emotions. Major intervals are bright and strong, emotionally, while minor intervals are weaker emotionally.

Augmented fourth - violence, danger, diabolus in musica.
Perfect fifth - stability, joy, triumph, heroics, courage.
Minor sixth - hardness, feeling lost.
Major third - sweetness, pleasure.

4. Musical Scales
We compose music using musical scales. A scale is a set of notes, placed apart from each other by specific intervals for this given scale. There are many different scales and they may have different intervals, different number of scales and so on, but most of the scales we use in Western music is made of seven notes.
In most cases, the notes of the scale are a semitone or two semitones apart from the previous note or next note. Some oriental scales use quarter tones, too. If you press any key on a piano keyboard, and then you press the key right next to it (either white or black, whichever is closer), you have heard a semitone. By now you already know what semitones are, because you’ve learned how to count the intervals.
Again - scales are sets of specific notes.

Let’s discuss one other thing that you may have noticed. Scales C Major and A Minor contain the same notes, but they start with a different first note (called a key). C Major starts with C and goes up, while scale A Minor starts with A and goes up. When we have this kind of situation we say that these scales are relative to each other. A relative scale can be quickly identified using a so-called circle of fifths.
Relative Scales
A circle of fifths is a musical tool that helps us see relations between the notes and keys. A typical wheel of fifths show all possible key notes and their flats and sharps symbols. Take a look at figure 4.5, where the outer letters are major keys (major scales, for example), and the inner letters are their relative minor keys.
Figure 4.5
By the way, a key is also known as the tonic - the very first note of a given scale.
How to use the wheel of fifths? First locate the major scale key (the outer letters), and then you look at the related key in the minor, inner ring of letters. It works the other way around, too. For example, relative scale for C Major is the A Minor scale, while relative scale for F# Minor is A Major scale.
Relative scales have the same notes, but a different tonic - a different key, or in other words, the very first note that starts the scale. This fact also changes the purpose of each note and the chords built with these notes - but more on that later.
It is possible to figure out relative scales without the wheel of fifths. If you want to find a relative minor scale, its tonic will be the sixth note of the base major scale. If you want to find a major scale, its tonic will be the third note of the base minor scale. In even simpler words, to identify the relative minor scale, count three semitones down from the base tonic - for example, from C down to A. To find a relative major scale, count three semitones up from the tonic, like from A up to C.

How to tell minor scale from major scale, if we have relative scales with all the same notes? It’s possible and I will explain this in just a moment. For now know this: you’ve just learned two basic scales: minor and major. These are the types of scales that can be built upon any of the 12 chromatic notes as key.
For example, C Major is a major scale built upon note C, and this note is called a key (the first note of a scale is always called either a key or a tonic). A Minor is a minor scale built upon note A. Because we have 12 different notes (seven basic notes, and five chromatic notes with sharps or flats), we may have 12 different keys. There are other types of scales, as well, like pentatonic scales. When you know what a scale is (a set of notes built with a specific pattern of intervals), it is easy to read and learn new scales. Right now, let’s learn how to actually build minor and major scales.
We use intervals and semitones to build our scales. Because of the pattern of these semitones, every major scale sounds the same, despite the fact that it is built using a different key.
Major Scale
For major scales, the pattern looks like this: WWHWWWH, and W is a whole tone, H is a half tone. Whole tone is also known as the whole step. Half tone is also known as half step or semitone. Usually, a whole tone equals two keys on a piano keyboard, either black or white, and half tone equals only a single key. We may also use digits: 2212221 - these are the “steps” measured in semitones. Let’s take a look at a major scale and count the semitones.

With this simple pattern, you can build a major scale in any given key, any of the 12 chromatic notes. Try it yourself, and build an A Major scale.
Right now is a good time to mention that every single one of these seven notes of either minor or major scale has its name, the degree of the scale.
First note/degree - tonic (the first degree of the scale that defines the name of the scale, like C in C Major). It’s often called a key.
Second note/degree - supertonic.
Third note/degree - mediant.
Fourth note/degree - subdominant.
Fifth note/degree - dominant.
Sixth note/degree - submediant.
Seventh note/degree - leading tone (or subtonic in minor scales)
Later on, this knowledge of tonics, dominants or mediants will become quite useful to work with chords and to create chord progression. Know that no matter if we work with major, minor or modal scale with seven notes, these seven notes always have the same names and thus, same functions.
The names of these notes are all based on the central note - the tonic, and thus they are derived from this note. Normally, you cannot see it, so we have to rearrange these notes a bit on a staff. Take a look at the figure below:
Notice that the tonic note is placed in the very center. This is the tonic center, the key of the scale, and everything revolves around it, it is the center of the song you’re writing in a given scale.
Every Major and Minor scale has three main notes: the tonic, the dominant and the subdominant. The tonic can be considered the center of the scale. The dominant chord is always a fifth higher than the tonic. And the subdominant chord is always a fifth lower than the tonic. This is the reason behind the name “subdominant”. “Sub” means “below”, but it’s not “below dominant note”, but more of a “dominant note below the tonic”. See it yourself in the figures above.
The second degree is called a supertonic, as it lies right above the tonic. For the same reason, the note just below the tonic is called subtonic - it lies below. But subtonic is often reserved for minor scales, and in major scales it is more often called a “leading tone”. I’ll explain why soon.
The third note is called a mediant (“in the middle” in Latin), it lies between the tonic and the dominant note. The sixth note is called submediant, as it lies between tonic and subdominant.
The only exception from “the same names rule” is the seventh note - in major scales it is called a leading tone, it has a strong need to lead to tonic note (this means it produces tension in music that wants to be resolved by a tonic note) - in major scales, there is no other note between a leading tone and tonic - no white key, no black key. But in minor scales, between the leading tone and the tonic, there’s a single chromatic note. Thus, in minor scales, the seventh note is no longer a leading tone, and has no need to be resolved to a tonic. Instead, it is called a subtonic and has a slightly different function.
This small difference of a subtonic affects how we create harmonies and build chords in minor scales, but this is a bit more advanced topic, so we won’t discuss it now.
When you hit the eight note, you notice it’s the very same note as the tonic, just an octave higher.
Now you know how to build a major scale - you got the pattern of semitones. Emotionally, major scales are very bright, happy, positive. They are often used to create more positive music.

Natural Minor Scale
The basic minor scale is called a natural minor scale. We build a natural minor scale with the following pattern: WHWWHWW, or 2122122.
But why “natural” minor scale? Well, we actually have three minor scales, and the natural minor scale is the first of the three. You’ve already learned about one of such natural scales, when you learned A Minor.

There are two other minor scales: a harmonic minor scale and melodic minor scale.
Harmonic Minor Scale
A harmonic minor scale is a direct result of natural minor. The only difference is the seventh note, which in harmonic minor is a semitone higher. This is the pattern for harmonic minor scale: 2122131. The 3 in this pattern is our seventh note.
Figure 4.11
Harmonic minor scale, thanks to this higher seventh note, sounds a bit orient, exotic.
This scale is often used for the purpose of chords - the seventh note allows us to create a major dominant chord in a minor scale (something not possible in a natural minor scale), and this allows us to create a traditional chord progression, in which a dominant is resolved to a tonic. It’s a bit more advanced knowledge, but basically you have to change the dominant minor chord into dominant major, by changing the third of the chord by a semitone up (raised seventh). Just know that you may encounter a raised seventh degree in minor scales and in this case you know you’re looking at a harmonic minor scale.
Melodic Minor Scale
The final minor scale explained in this book is the melodic minor scale. In this scale, both sixth and seventh notes are a bit higher than in natural minor. But beware! We make these two notes higher only when the scale goes up. When it goes down, we go back to the natural minor scale. Figure 4.12 illustrates this.
This is the pattern for melodic minor scale: 2122221.
Figure 4.12
When the melodic minor goes up, we raise the sixth and seventh notes of this scale, but when the scales go down, we cancel these two notes, and we use the good old natural minor scale.
Now you know three basic minor scales and you know the patterns of semitones to build them.
Musical Scales in Practice
Now that you know how to build the basic musical scales, you need to learn how to use them in practice.
This is rather simple - at least the basics are simple. The entire music piece is made only with the notes that belong to a given scale. For example, if you compose a piece in A Minor, you will use only notes A, B, C, D, E, F, and G. You will note use any chromatic notes, not for melody, nor for chords.
When music is made only of the notes of a given scale, we say that this music is diatonic.
When you learn more about music, you may want to start using notes outside the scale (so called non-diatonic notes), but this is a bit more advanced topic and we won’t discuss it right now. Anyway, know two things: first, a lot of music is diatonic, and that’s perfectly fine. Second, when studying music you may notice that some composers use non-diatonic notes, too - and that’s fine, too.

Modes and Emotions
Scales have their own “feel to them” and thus they can shape specific emotions. Here are some keywords for each of the modes:
Ionian - Bright, positive, stable, uplifting. This mode is the basic major scale and it’s associated with very positive music.
Dorian - Sad. This is often used in Celtic music, or American folk music of the old days. It may sound melancholic, or introspective in some manner. It may be used to compose irish-like folk music.
Phrygian - Mystical, darker. This mode is often encountered in flamenco music, and it has a bit middle-eastern feel to it, keep this in mind when writing simple desert-like soundtracks.
Lydian - Exotic, etheric, comfortable. This is a popular scale used in jazz.
Mixolydian - Happy. Now this one is more blues-like.
Aeolian - Melancholic. A natural minor scale. This time, this melancholy here is not introspective, but it’s like it’s despairing.
Locrian - Unstable, different, uncomfortable. This mode is more “theoretical”, and it’s difficult to work with, but it may be used in some heavy metal music.
Like in the case of the intervals, in case of modes emotions can be quite subjective as well, but some of these emotions will be common among various people. If you want to convey specific emotions with your music, you may want to pick up a specific mode.
These various modes work because of different intervals and relationship between these intervals - this is what shapes different moods and feels. Also, these intervals create different chords, so we can play around with these, as well.
And you can use these modes interchangeably by borrowing some chords from each other. This will be explained in the second part of this book in the section about modal interchange.

5. Chords
Finally, it is time to learn about chords. Chords are important because they can help you shape the harmonious and emotional texture of your music.
A chord is made of three or more notes that are played either at the same time, or in a specific pattern. In other words, you play chords by pressing three or more keys on a piano at the same time (or playing them using a midi grid in the editor).
Later on, you’ll learn that chords can be played as arpeggios patterns, meaning note after note - more on that later.
Some people say that a chord is already made of two notes and then it is called a dyad. Figure 5.1 shows this.
Figure 5.1
Dyads, two notes that sound at the same time, in my opinion can be called chords (and they definitely imply a chord, or suggest a chord that lacks some notes), but even more than that, they are a great example of harmony - harmony occurs when two or more notes are played at the same time. Harmony is a term that refers to the relation between two or more notes, which relation shapes emotional content of your music.
These two or more sounds can be consonant and pleasant, or dissonant and unpleasant. You already know what consonants and dissonants are. I’ve mentioned this when we talked about the intervals. Chords are made with specific intervals, too. Chords can be major, minor, diminished or augmented.
Let’s learn a typical chord now. This one, shown in figure 5.2, is a triad, made of three notes.
Figure 5.2
A chord on figure 5.2 is a C Major (yes, the name is similar to the name of the scale). Triads are the most common chords in music. Let’s discuss them now. Specifically, let’s discuss major and minor triads.
Each triad is made of three notes, and these notes have their names: a root, a third, a fifth. You already know these last two words - they are the intervals. In reality, in Western music, chords are made of thirds and thirds alone. The third is a third to the root, but the fifth is a third to the third. What does it mean? Take a look at figure 5.2 again.
Between the root, the lowest note, and the highest note, you can see seven semitones - this is a perfect fifth. Between the root and the middle note, there are four semitones - this is a major third. And between the middle note and the highest note, there are three semitones - it’s a minor third. A chord made of four notes, called a seventh chord is a chord with an additional third above the ordinary fifth. In other words, chords are made of thirds.
Let’s go back to basic triads.
The root, the lowest note, is the base of the chords and it gives the chord its name. For C Major chord, note C is its root. Its third is the note E (major third) and its fifth is note G. Chords are a sum of their intervals.
Each triad is made of a root, a third and a fifth. The fifth remains unchanged - I mean that in case of major triads and minor triads, the fifth is always a perfect fifth. But the third moves - it can be a major or minor compared to root, and this changes the chord from minor to major. Figure 5.3 shows a C Minor triad. Chords Symbols in Literature
In books, chords are written down in various ways.
Major chords may be represented by a roman numeral, or with an additional “maj” written down, like G, or Cmaj
Minor chords are usually written down with an “m”, like Gm
Diminished may have a circle to them or a word “dim”, like Gdim, or G○
Augmented may have an “aug” written down, or a + sign, like Gaug, or G+
For example: C, Cmaj, A, Am, Bdim, E+. When we have seventh chords, we also add a numeral, for example: C7, Cmaj7, Am7, Bdim7 and so on. This digit stands for the fourth note of the chord. Suspended chords use the sus word and a digit, sus2 for suspended second or sus4 for suspended fourth. You will learn about suspended chords later.
But sometimes, you may get lost - like when Cmaj7 is not seventh chord C Major, but a C major chord with a major seventh. This is more advanced knowledge that fits perfectly in books about music harmony, so don’t worry about this now.
Just remember that we write down chords in various ways. But when you make music on your computer, this shouldn’t be a problem too often.
Finally, the general rule is that chords are represented by roman numerals, with uppercase I, II, III, IV, V, VI, VII for major chords, and lowercase i, ii, iii, iv, v, vi, vii for minor chords - this rule is much more universal.

Chord Inversions
The notes in the chords can switch places, like when the third goes at the bottom, as an example. We call this chord inversions. We change the place of a root, a third or a fifth, but at the same time, we do not change the function of these notes.
The basic position of a chord is known as the root position. In it, a root is at the bottom, a third is in the middle, and a fifth is at the top. Figure 5.14 shows a C Major chord in root position.
Figure 5.14
Inversions allows us to change the placement of the chord’s notes. We usually use these inversions to make our chord sound different, but inversions are often used for proper voice leading in chord progressions. Basically, in chord progressions the notes of each chord should move around as little as possible, and this is achieved through inversions. We will discuss chord progressions and voice movement later.
Before we learn inversions, you need to remember one thing that I mentioned a few seconds ago: the function of the note remains the same. So even if the root note is at the top of the chord, it still remains a root. A third at the very bottom of the chord remains a third.
All right, let’s learn about two basic inversions:
First inversion - we move the root at the very top of the chord.
Second inversion - we move the third to the very top. Second inversion occurs after the first, so if you want to move the third to the top, the root must be already there, and then the fifth is at the bottom, and root is in the middle.
If there is no inversion, we say that the chord is in its root position.

Figure 5.15
As you can see on figure 5.15, we have moved the root to the top. This is the first inversion. Of course, you can also move the root two or three octaves up - this, too, will be the first inversion of the chord.

For example, if you work with C Major scale, and you have notes G, E and C, you know it’s an inverted C Major chord, because you can’t build any other chord with these notes in this scale.
Inversions can be quite useful when composing music, because inverted chords have a different sound, so you can add some interest to your music. Also, inversions help with voicing - what is voicing?
Voicing and Doubling
When you change the position of the notes in the chord, or you spread the notes between various octaves, we call this voicing.
The chord does not have to fit a single octave. For example, a third can be moved an octave higher, or even two octaves higher. Let’s take a look at figure 5.18 showing a C Major chord in inversions and with different spread.
Figure 5.18 - OGG Included.
On the left, we have a close voicing - the notes are very near each other, in the range of the same octave. But we can also have an open voicing, like in the case of all the rest of the chords on figure 5.18, when notes are spread over many octaves.
Voicing is very important for composition - we spread the notes to different octaves to fill the entire spectrum of the sound - the low, the middle and the high parts of the spectrum (or, as you will learn later: soprano, alto, tenor and bass). Later on, when you assign instruments to different notes, a proper voicing will result in a clear composition and clear sound. And when the notes and chords follow each other as the music progresses, we also have the rules for how these voices move - you may encounter this topic in music guidebooks, and I will discuss this as well in part II.
As you can hear with example from figure 5.18, an open voicing like in the second chord from the left doesn’t sound right. To make it sound good, you need to work on the voicing and double some of the voices - in other words, double some of the notes.
When the notes are placed near each other within a range of a normal interval and a single octave, we call this a close voicing. But when we spread the notes into two or more octaves, we call this an open voicing.
The notes in the chord can be doubled - this means repeated in a different octave. For example, you may have four notes in a chord, but two of these notes are a C, in two different octaves. This means a C note has been doubled. Take a look at C Major on figure 5.18 (on the right), listen to it and you should notice that it sounds a lot better.
Here are the basic rules for doubling notes of the chord:
The root is doubled the most - on figure 5.18 we have 4 notes C, and in C Major this note is a root.
Fifth is next to be doubled - I doubled it 2 times. In a good voicing, you can double the fifth, but there should be more doubled roots than fifths, yet less thirds than fifths.
Third is the last to be doubled in a triad, and in case of sevenths, ninth s and other bigger chords, these additional notes are doubled even less frequently.
An exception in case of thirds happens in minor scales, when the third can be doubled more freely, as it doesn’t break the sound of the chord that much. Wrong doubling will make the chord sound “weak”, and may also weaken its emotional impact.
We double the notes like this because the root is the foundation of the chord and it needs to be very clear sounding. A fifth is also an important part of the chord, but we can’t double it too much compared to the root, because too many fifths would hide the sound of the root. Finally, a third is needed to set the minority or majority of the chord, but if you put too many thirds in your voicing, the entire chord will become blurred. Bad voicing (wrong doubling) changes the sound of the chord for worse.
Listen to the chord from figure 5.19. The chord on the left has a nice voicing, while the chord on the right - the same chord - has a bad voicing.
Figure 5.19 - OGG Included.
A chord on the right doesn’t sound right.
You need to keep these rules of doubling in mind when composing music. Also, you should only double the root in the lower octaves - too much doubling of the fifth or third in the bass frequencies (low octaves) will damage the sound of the chord.
The higher the octave is, the closer the voicing. So in higher octaves, you can put notes a lot closer together.

6. Chord Functions and Chord Progressions
It is time to learn about chord functions - the roles they play in the entire musical piece. With this knowledge you’ll be able to learn about chord progressions. By now you should know that we use roman numerals for each of the seven chords, built upon seven degrees of the scale. For example, we have chords I, ii, iii, IV, V, vi vii in major scales. The uppercase roman numerals represent major chords, and lowercase roman numerals represent minor chords.
There are seven chords in a major and minor scale, because we build each chord upon a single degree of a scale - a single note. And there are seven notes in a typical minor and major scale.
An augmented chord is usually marked with a + sign, and a diminished chord is usually represented by a circle next to a numeral, or “dim” word. That said, there are rarely any augmented chords in normal scales.

Minor scales have the same degrees (i, II, III and so on...), and in any scale we start with a roman numeral I - that represents the tonic function of a chord. In every scale, whether major or minor, and no matter what chromatic note this scale starts with, we start with chord I and end up on VII, and every single chord on a particular degree has the same function. For example, chord I in scale C Major is a tonic, and chord I in A Minor is also a tonic. The numeral and function and the name of the chord remains the same. Only the notes change. Every chord built on the fifth degree of a scale is a V - Dominant chord. Every chord built on a second degree of a scale is a II - Supertonic chord.
Each of the seven chords has a name:
I - Tonic
II - Supertonic
III - Mediant
IV - Subdominant
V - Dominant
VI - Submediant
VII - Leading tone (or subtonic in minor scales)
And each of these chords has a “function” - it plays a specific role in a chord progression, the underlying structure in music. This is where we start talking about harmony.
We have three main chords - I, IV and V - tonic, subdominant and dominant chords. These three chords, together, contain all the notes of a scale. For example, in C Major a tonic is made of C, E, G; dominant is G, B, D; subdominant is F, A, C; in total, we have all the notes of the C Major scale. With these three chords, we can harmonize any kind of melody written in C Major. Harmonization is a process of adding chords to a melody - the chords should fit the melody, this is a basic rule.
We have four other chords: II, III, VI and VII. Each of these chords finds its place in a chord progression. A chord progression is a… well, progression of chords, one after another. Each chord in a chord progression can lead to a couple of other chords and the rules that say what chord leads where, help us build chord progressions that sound good. For example, the chord I is usually the first chord in a chord progression, and it often leads to chord V, which may lead to VI, which may lead to IV, which leads again to I and everything starts again.

This is a chord progression - not necessary in I, II, III… order, but in an order that sounds good. The chords change in a regular fashion (called a harmonic rhythm), and upon these chords we build melodies, thus creating music.

As I said, we have three main chords - I, IV and V. We may add chords II, III, VI and VII between them, or we may substitute these three main chords with four additional chords, because these three also represent groups:
I, III and VI - think of as a tonic group.
II, IV - subdominant group.
V, VII - dominant group.
Each of these chords has a function. Tonic is a stable chord, the beginning and a satisfactory end. It may start and end the piece. All other chords surround the tonic, and the tonic is the harmonic center of the musical piece.
A mediant and a submediant are similar to tonic, because they share a single note with a tonic chord. They may act as a substitution for tonic.
Chords VI and II lead away from the tonic, musically they lead to a different place. They are used if we want to leave the tonic behind for a moment. But they also lead to the V chord, the dominant.
Chord IV is a good, stable chord that often leads back to the tonic.
We also have chords V and VII - they are unstable, tensed and they must be resolved somehow - usually by returning to the tonic chord.

Chords for Relative Keys
Perhaps you remember when we talked about relative scales - if they have the same notes, we emphasize the key of the scale to clearly define our scale. I mentioned that chords can be used for the same purpose, to place an emphasis on the key and type of the scale.
What about the chords? These, too, can be substituted or replaced. In relative scales we always have the same chords. This means an A Minor is always an A Minor chord, both in C Major scale and in A Minor scale. But in C major it’s a sixth chord, and in A Minor, it’s the first chord. So the function changes. Harmonizing a Song
Harmonizing a song is all about adding chords to a melody - we can add entire chords, or parts of a chord (like dyads, that only suggest/imply a chord). By entire chords I mean adding triads, for example. And by parts of a chord, I mean notes of the chord that suggest the chord - for example, adding only the root and the fifth, but omitting the third.
How to add a chord to a song? A couple of techniques exist. Usually you create a melody in a given key and given scale - this already tells you what kind of chords you’re dealing with, because these chords are built upon seven degrees of a scale in a given key. When you have a melody composed by someone else and you don’t know the key, take a look at the very first note of the melody - sometimes it’s the first note of the scale used (but not always, so be careful). Or take a look at which note is the lowest, or which note is most often used in the melody - these are not the rules, but sometimes you can figure out the scale and key this way.
When you know the and the scale, you know the chords available to you. Next, take a look at the notes in a given bar that you wish to harmonize. What kind of notes are there in this bar? Usually, we harmonize the melody with a chord containing at least one note of the melody line. Sometimes, you see that note A is dominant in a melody in a particular bar - you have a couple of chords to use in such a case, as a couple of them contain note A. Sometimes, the melody contain both notes A and E, and a couple additional notes, but you know that chord A Minor contains both A and E, so it can be used to harmonize the melody.

The basics of composition and harmony
8. Music Composition - Introduction and Melody
By now you know the building blocks of music: notes, intervals, chords and scales. It is time to explain how to put these building blocks together and learn how to compose music. While music composition is a huge topic, I will provide you with the basics - not too little, so you can create quite complex music, but not too much either, so you won’t get overwhelmed.
Music composition will be explained in a couple of chapters. First, we will learn how to create melodies. This will include explaining motifs, phrases and themes.
Then, we will learn more about harmony - chords are not that simple after all. We will discuss how to compose with chords, and how to add chords to your melodies. We will also discuss voicing and the movement of four voices. Finally, we will discuss the form of music and explain various ways we can put our compositions together.
Teaching composition is difficult. So I will take you step by step through a process I personally choose.
What is Music Composition
Music composition is taking music blocks such as notes and chords and putting them together into a whole that makes sense to the composer and the listener. This “whole” is meant to lead somewhere, like to a specific emotion, or resolution, or meaning; or it is meant to use harmonic content (emotional content) to support another medium, such as a game or film. Every composer understands composition differently.
But basically, you have a goal - your music should mean something - and you use musical elements such as notes and chords and scales to achieve your goal. The goal is often difficult to define, and our discussion here may feel abstract.
Sometimes you just want to create a trance piece that is 8 minutes long and feels uplifting. Sometimes you want to create a 3 minutes song made of verses and chorus to convey your emotions through words and harmonic content. Sometimes you have a film scene that is 15 seconds long and the emotions this scene represents must be additionally supported with music. And sometimes you need exactly 57 seconds of happy commercial music because someone offers you a lot of money for their ad.
Sometimes, to achieve your goal you need to come up with a melody and some chords and some structure. Other times, your desired music is static, it doesn’t go anywhere, it’s just there. It may or may not have a melody, it may be an ambient piece. Sometimes, desired music is very dynamic, like in film scores, and it changes constantly.
In all these cases, you start with nothing - a blank page, a blank mind, or a blank workspace in your DAW. The job is simple: fill in the blanks. Create music out of nothing.
How to start? The answer to this question is quite difficult. Some people start with a melody. Others start with the entire chord progression. Others think of form. Some people start with a single chord that gives them an emotional feeling that, well, feels “right” for their specific goal.
But there are some simple steps you can follow to create your composition. Although teaching music composition can be quite abstract, I can show you more building blocks of music such as motifs and themes and forms. With this, you’ll be able to start, and then the more music you actually compose the easier to understand all of this becomes.
Let’s say music is like talking. You use letters (notes) to create words (motifs), and you use words to create phrases (musical phrases). Then you use these phrases to express ideas and opinions, along with emotions (chords and harmony), thus creating discussion topics (musical themes).
Let’s start.
Let’s say you already have a melody you came up with - open the MIDI editor and type the melody in. You may need to adjust the time signature and tempo in your DAW to match the melody. With the melody ready, you’ll be able to add some harmony to it. This will be covered later. Also later you will learn about various forms of music - once you know the form, it’s much easier to build upon it. For example, if you want to create a pop song, just create a chord progression for the verse (the A section), another chord progression for the chorus (the B section), and then build your song as ABAB form. You’re done.
But what if you do not have a melody? Well, now we can learn about more building blocks of music. But before we do, we need to make some more decisions. For example, we need to choose a key and the scale.
Choosing the Key and the Scale
First, you should choose a scale (we also say what we are picking a key ). Two basic scales we can work with are major and minor scales. You already have a couple of scales to choose from. They each have their own mood, the “feel”, or the emotional content. Pick up the one that fits the idea you want to convey. The basic generalization would be to pick up a major scale for happy song, and minor scale for sad song. But unfortunately, this isn’t so easy.
(If you already have a melody, you need to figure out what scale it is. Type the melody into your DAW and compare its notes with the scales from this guide. You may need to move some of them up and down through various keys to find the scale that fits.)
For example, a lot of uplifting trance music is actually written in a minor scale - the music feels positive, but the scale is minor. Don’t worry if you don’t understand why or how - it comes with experience. The more music you produce, the easier it is to intuitively understand how to work with scales.
Try to listen to all the scales I provided for this book and learn how they “feel” to you, then pick up the scale you think is the best for your idea, and then choose a key in which this particular scale feels right to you.
With a scale and a key chosen, you can start experimenting with a melody.
Beginners should stick to a simple rule: for each of your compositions, use only the notes of the particular scale in the particular key. So if you choose a major scale in the key of C, use only notes C, D, E, F, G, A and B. This is a diatonic approach to composition. If you’d like to use notes from outside the scale, this would mean you would take a chromatic approach. Diatonic notes are the notes of the scale, and chromatic notes are the notes outside this chosen scale.
To me, choosing the key is interconnected with the melody I came up with. Once I figure out a nice motif or an entire melody, I like to move it up or down the MIDI grid and listen to it, to get the feel of this key. This is one approach. Sometimes I simply press a key on my keyboard and I can hear the note I like for a particular melody.
Modes
Some keys feel different than the others. For example, you’ve learned about major and minor scales and their “modes” - they’re all scales built on the white keys on the piano keyboard. Each mode has a different “feel” to it - you can choose different modes to fit your songs. Please go back to the earlier section about modes, where I’ve provided you with some keywords and suggestions on the “feel” of modes.
As I said previously, modes use different intervals and thus allow us to create different chords. This is what makes them feel different from each other. Still, all the modes allow us to create tonal music which has a tonal center (its chosen key) and still sounds familiar to most listeners.
Once you get familiar with tonal music and composition, you may be interested to know that there are other scales, as well. Some even use quarter tones, like in Middle Eastern music. Or they use different intervals. I won’t describe this here, but know that if you ever need to compose Middle Eastern Music, Hindu music or Japanese music (these are only a few examples), you should study this type of music, as it may use different scales, intervals and tones. By learning traditional elements of such music, you will be able to mimic the style better.
Relative Keys and Composing
Remember that every major key music can be made minor key - once you know the key of your melody, you can take a look at the Wheel of Fifths and find the relative key either to the major or minor key you’re using. This could be very useful. First of all, you don’t want to change the notes you’re using, just the “mode”.
For example, you have a nice, happy melody for the verse of your song, and for some reason you want to make the chorus use the same melody, but in a sad mode. All you have to do is to look for the relative minor to your major key and change the chords.
The same goes for scoring games or films. You may already have a nice theme or a leitmotif - to make it sound positive, you can use major scale, and to make it sound sad, you can use minor scales. You can switch between scales using the relative feature of the Wheel of Fifths. The notes are the same, the scale (and chords) changes. The chords are the key - in most cases they are the ones that change between major and minor relative keys.
There’s a different trick composers use when it comes to switching between minor and major scales. Instead of using a relative key, they keep the same key, they just make it the opposite. For example, some parts of the songs are written in C Major, and other parts are written in C Minor - some notes may need to be changed (because the scales don’t mirror each other’ notes), and you may need to add some sharps and flats, but that’s OK. This simple technique is used when your particular composition at some point requires a different “mood” than the rest of the song.

Anyway, once you have a scale and mode, you know that the melody and chords will be built with the notes of this scale and no other (chromatic, that is outside the scale) notes.
For the purpose of this guide, I will use mainly two scales: major one and natural minor.
We’re going to learn about some basic ideas behind music: notes that can create motifs. Motifs can be used in melodies and to create phrases. Phrases can be turned into periods and themes, and thus create the form.

Melody & Rhythm
It’s time to create some melody. Melody is a set of notes played in succession, built upon specific musical elements such as chords and scales. Melody is basically made of two things: a motif that is a part of a phrase. As such, these notes are contained within a specific number of beats, creating sections, and these sections can then repeat themselves - either with or without any changes. Here we can utilize a powerful concept in music: repetition.
But first, let’s start with the smallest musical element - the motif.
Motif
A motif is the shortest piece of musical content you can come up with. The idea is that a motif is something that is easily recognized throughout the piece. And it’s repetitive, like a short repetitive phrase that is easy to recognize.
A motif can be a single note. Or it can be two notes. Or it can be three notes. Or it can be a very short melody. Motif can be melodic or rhythmic - even playing the same note over again in a specific rhythm will become a motif. In any of these cases, it must be easily recognized throughout the piece.
While a motif is often associated with a melody, a motif can also be harmonic in nature, like a specific chord, or specific chord progression. A motif can also be instrumental in nature, like using a specific instrument or texture to represent the motif.
But the most obvious one is the melodic motif. Once you have the scale and key selected, start playing around with the notes and come up with a very short phrase that you like. This will be much easier when you listen to many different songs and pick up the motifs they use.

Leitmotif
A different variation of a motif is a leitmotif - this is a motif that is associated with a character or event, and it is often used in operas, film scores, and video game scores. A leitmotif is not necessarily a short thing - while motifs can be as simple as two notes, a leitmotif can be longer. But even two notes will serve as a leitmotif if they’re clearly associated with something.
Leitmotifs work well in film scores or video game scores - as such, they are used throughout the entire score in various places. It’s almost a cliche to say it, but the most popular leitmotif in popular culture would be a couple of notes from the Imperial March by John Williams, that listeners associate with the character of Darth Vader.
Leitmotifs are melodies - and they’re a framework, upon which you can build various moods. For example, the same leitmotif can be harmonized with happy chords and sad chords. This is useful when in a film score you have a character portrayed in a happy scene and a sad scene, and you want to use the same leitmotif in both scenes.
To create a leitmotif, simply create a motif or an entire short melody and use it whenever a specific person, place or event appears on the screen, to create a mental connection between the music and the event.
Developing Melody
Now we can talk a bit more about melody.
Melody - the thing you clearly hear in music and you’re capable of singing later - can work on its own, or can be supported by chords and instrumentations.
But how do you come up with a melody? There are many techniques. I suggest reading this chapter first, because many things overlap here, so it will be easier to develop melodies once you learn a couple of techniques.
First of all, when you have successfully developed the motif, you can then expand it with additional notes to come up with a nice melody, or at least part of the melody. You can expand the motif simply by playing on the keyboard around the motif, and see what you can come up with.
There is a popular technique in music to try and match the notes on the staff to the things you can see with your eyes. For example, you see a mountain range, so you try to recreate the contour of the mountains on a staff with notes. You can try this out, but personally I never had any luck with this technique outside my hobby of playing Native American style flutes. Some people like this technique, though, so I wanted to mention it in this book.
So I would like you to show three techniques, similar to each other:
Simply play random keys on a keyboard - either “in empty space”, or around the motif if you already have one.
Or press a single key on a keyboard and see where you can go from there.
Play a particular scale over and over again until you come up with an inspiration.
This is just simple improvisation. It can actually inspire you. As you press the keys, you may develop a melody that you like, a melody that conveys your ideas or emotions. Come up with random notes, and random ideas. Try to develop the ideas you like further either by adding notes, removing notes, or changing the rhythm of the melody. Here, the best advice is this: improvise melodic and generally musical material on any instrument you want. Piano, guitar, harmonica, flute, whatever you like!
(If you already have a melody in your head, or in your DAW, you can use these techniques to develop the melody further into a phrase or a theme).
Developing melodies from scales can be very useful for beginners. I learned this technique years ago, when playing Native American style flute - it has only five notes of minor pentatonic scale, so all the melodies must fit this particular scale. Just play around the notes of the scale and see if you can come up with something you like. Somewhere in this process, you may also come up with a nice core rhythm of your song.
(As you can see, there’s a lot of “see what you can come up with”. This is because once you have the scale, and a motif, or at least the scale, everything else is just a matter of bringing all musical elements together, and this is quite difficult to explain. But you will understand all of this once you start working with a DAW and/or a piano keyboard.)
Composing with scales is very useful when you know what kind of emotions you want to convey through your music. If they’re happy, you may want to use a major scale. Sad music benefits from minor scales. And there are many more complex emotions associated with various scales - we call these scales: modes. Like I said earlier, choose a scale or mode, play the notes of this scale/mode, and try to develop melodies. You can do this by using a motif, or without it; by using a rhythm that you like, or without it.
Also, remember that every scale and mode can be placed on any of 12 chromatic notes, and every one of these notes - these keys - will change the scale/mode a bit. So if you like the melody, but you’re unsure if the key is right, simply move the melody up or down the piano roll and see if this changes things.
When we talk about developing melodies with a scale, we need to talk about selecting a key - you pick up a key and a scale. And you pick up a tonic - usually the key note, the first note of the scale you have chosen. Your music - both the melody and the chord - will then be composed around the tonic, which means the tonic will be present, this particular note will be present in the chords, and in the melody. The melody usually starts with the tonic, and ends with the tonic. It’s like a main focus of your music, and thus a term tonality is born - in this book we talk about tonal music, music that is centered around a tonic note.
Without the tonic, your music will sound like it doesn’t go anywhere, it’s chaotic, it lacks foundation and purpose.
Thus, you pick up a key and scale, and you pick your tonic. Then you create a melody that is centered around the tonic, and uses rhythm and intervals to make sense to the listener.
Which tonic can you choose? You know there are 12 notes - and for every of these notes you can choose any of the scales you have learned. Thus you’re not limited to A Minor, or C Major. You can compose in F# Minor, or B Major, or pick up any of the modes you’ve learned in any key. Whenever you choose the key, this key - this main note - is your tonic.
***
Don’t be afraid to use various keys for various scales, because each key in each scale will result in different “feel” and mood. Christian Schubart wrote a wonderful book, Ideen zu einer Aesthetik der Tonkunst , in which he described some key characteristics, since before the XX century, associating different keys with different emotions was quite popular. For example:
C Major - innocence and simplicity
C Minor - love, unhappy love, longing
Db Major - grief, weak small, unhappiness
C# Minor - lament, conversation with God, sad love
D Major - triumph, victory, heroism
D Minor - melancholy
Eb Major - love, conversation with God
D# Minor - anxiety, distress, fear
E Major - joy, delight
E Minor - innocence, tears, sadness
F Major - calmness
F Minor - sadness, depression, misery
F# Major - triumph, conquering the struggle
F# Minor - resentment
G Major - idyllic, calmness, tenderness, gentleness
G Minor - worries, resentment, dislike
Ab Major - death
Ab Minor - lament, struggle, difficulty
A Major - innocent love, hope
A Minor - tenderness
Bb Major - hope and happiness
Bb Minor - disconnection
B Major - passion, anger, fury
B Minor - patience, calmness
If you can find Schubart’s work, you can learn even more about these key characteristics.
***
Somewhere in the melody, you can also add a dominant note of the scale you’ve chosen. The dominant note is the fifth degree of the chosen scale - like in chords, the tonic chord likes to lead to the dominant chord, and dominant chord likes to be resolved with the tonic chord - the same goes for melodies. The melody can create arcs and move from tonic to dominant, and from dominant to tonic note. This is a general concept, and you can utilize it in any way you want. Sometimes you will use this approach, sometimes note - as long as your composition makes musical sense, it’s all right.
Then, you build your music with intervals, using steps and leaps. Step is a movement up or down between the two notes that is a semitone or tone apart. Leap is a movement bigger than a tone apart. And these movements can go up or down. Moving up usually adds energy to your music, and moving down usually removes this energy. Thus, moving up seems positive, and moving down seems depressed. But again, this can be quite subjective.
Do you remember when, in the chapter about intervals, I told you that intervals are often associated with emotions? For example, a perfect fifth interval is often associated with heroism. Composers use these emotional associations to create melodies. Thus, instead of just playing around the key, they already know what kind of intervals they want to add to their melody to convey specific emotions - they take these intervals to create motifs and then entire melodies in a more strategic fashion.
They can, for example, start a melody on C and then go to G (a perfect fifth interval) and then to some other notes in a given rhythm and that’s how they come up with a melody that conveys the feeling of heroism. You can use this heroic interval in a D Major key, that is quite heroic, you will get a very heroic piece of music. And this is something to remember when composing with steps and leaps.
Whenever you compose, you may try to think: what kind of note, interval, rhythm element, I associate with this particular idea or emotion? Think of your music like a story - every piece of the story can be represented by different musical ideas. This goes for melodies, chords and instrumentation.
This is a basic concept, but you should try this out - it makes composing specific melodies for film and game soundtracks much easier.
(By the way, did you know that a lot of composers start their songs with a title? They come up with a title and these few words can be enough to set up the mood of the song and thus point into the direction of chords and keys.)
Another way to create melodies is to think of music as human speech - as you talk, you intonate in various ways, and the tempo of speaking, as well as pitch of your voice, may change. Try to “speak” the melody - even if you’re composing instrumental music without any lyrics (you may try to come up with some words that represent the emotional idea you wish to convey through your music). See if you can “speak” the melody and write it down in your DAW.
Here’s a quick tip. These days, we all have access to smartphones and there are great apps we can use to record our musical ideas. Almost every smartphone comes with a voice recording app. Use it when you have an idea for a melody. Once back in your home studio, you can play the recorded humming (or even singing, if you’re up for a challenge) and write it down in your DAW. There are also mini-DAW programs you can install as apps on your smartphone, such as FL Studio Mobile.
I suggest you save all your musical ideas.
Composing Without an Instrument
You can compose music with a piano, or guitar, or any other instrument you like. But many computer composers, especially beginners, don’t own any instrument, and they use just a computer keyboard and a mouse to make music. This can be unfortunate, but not impossible. You can still add notes to the MIDI editor, move them around, play the melodies back and forth, and develop your musical ideas.
I actually compose a lot of my music this way, even if my MIDI keyboard is right there under the screen monitor, staring at me with a sad expression. Don’t worry if a mouse with a keyboard is your only way to compose music. It’s OK.
Don’t Be Afraid to Transpose
Don’t be afraid to compose everything in C Major or A Minor and then transpose your music to a different key. A lot of beginners find this technique useful. This way you can compose your piece using only the white keys on the piano roll. After you’re done, you can select all the notes, and just transpose them, that is: select all of them, and move them up or down the piano roll to a desired key.
Transposition also has a second meaning - it occurs when an instrument is notated on a staff on different notes than it sounds. For example, a Horn in F is written a fifth higher than it sounds. So if the staff shows a written note G, it will sound like C. I’m telling you this just so you know, for future reference. In most cases when you compose music in a DAW, you won’t have to worry about this kind of transposition.
(Transposition will be useful when you decide to become an even better composer and actually write down traditional scores to be recorded by an orchestra.)
Rhythm
At the same time as you develop your melody, you develop the core rhythm for this melody.
Rhythm is a part of our life. Everything around us has its own rhythm. Often, when you come up with a melody, you already come up with a rhythm for it. But sometimes you may need to develop rhythm on your own.
Rhythm may have a couple of layers:
Drum layer - the orchestral drums in cinematic score, or the kick with a hi-hats in EDM.
Melodic layer - the rhythmic layer of the melody that may fit the drum layer perfectly, or only change the melodic rhythm on specific notes, or even introduce syncopation from time to time.
Harmonic layer - the way the chords change.
Some rhythms come up to you naturally - one way or another, you’ve heard them before, even subconsciously, probably countless times by listening to music. Just write them down in your DAW and try to fit the melody to these rhythms.
If you have some kind of a drum (like an ethnic drum), or a rhythmic MIDI controller, that’s great! Use your hands or even fingers to experiment with various rhythms, simply by playing them live.
Every music composer should have an instrument to play with - you don’t have to be great at it! I recommend you learn how to play either a piano (even if it’s a MIDI keyboard with a virtual piano instrument), or an acoustic guitar, and some kind of drum or rhythmic MIDI controller (that you tap rhythmically, usually with your finger(s)). You don’t have to master these instruments - I would like you to learn how to play simple melodies and chords, and how to improvise rhythms. That’s it - learning a very basic set of skills such as these will allow you to listen to your musical ideas before you even type them into your DAW.
Another way to come up with a rhythm is to start writing a simple regular rhythm, for example on a strong beat. Listen to it on repeat and see what you can come up with
Try to fill the weak beat part of the bar with some other rhythmic elements - either regular or irregular.
Once again, you can start with a single note or beat, and develop the rhythmic idea through experimentation - listen and make changes, repeatedly if you have to. Adjust the rhythm to what you want to create. A sad song may need a slow rhythm. Ambient texture may need an even slower rhythm. Epic orchestral piece may need a fast, powerful rhythm.
Some genres already have established rhythms. For example, trance music uses a kick on a strong beat, and a double- or triple-bass on the weak beat of the bar. That’s the first rhythmic layer. Upon it, a melodic and harmonic rhythm is built. Ethnic music, like in India or the Middle East, has its own “rhythmic traditions”, just like many modern genres. When you decide to compose music in a specific genre, you need to learn about its “traditional” rhythm patterns.
A helpful tool is a drum loop - pre-recorded patterns that you can put on a track as audio files and be done with it. Such loops can be bought in online stores that sell packages for music producers. Sometimes, drum patterns come as MIDI files - you just load the MIDI file into your project, and add drum patches to these newly created tracks.
At the same time, you can use syncopation.
Syncopation occurs when we introduce change in an already established rhythm. Thus, first you establish the rhythm by repeating it through a couple of bars - let the listener get used to it. Then we can introduce syncopation by:
Moving some of the notes (or even a single note) left or right, thus slightly changing the melody.
Emphasizing a different beat than usual.
Tempo and Meter
Rhythm is strongly related to the time signature (meter) and tempo. Slow tempo equals slow rhythm, and fast tempo equals fast rhythm - this is a general rule that you may later on break. The general rule is this: positive (happy, energetic) songs use faster and more dynamic rhythms, while sad (ambient, contemplative) songs use slower and less dynamic rhythms.
(Obviously, the Duel of the Fates by John Williams is not really positive, but it uses a very fast tempo, so again, these are only general rules and suggestions.)
First, let’s talk about the tempo which we measure in BPM - Beats per M inute. I already discussed this in the first part of this book, but let’s repeat some general and popular tempos.
Largo : 40-60 BPM
Larghetto : 60-66 BPM
Adagio : 66-76 BPM
Andante : 76-108 BPM
Allegro : 108-168 BPM
Presto : 168-200 BPM
You can choose a tempo first, and then try to build some nice rhythm upon it.
The rhythm will also depend on the time signature, such as 4/4 or 3/4 or 6/8. Try to change the meter in your DAW and see if you can modify your rhythms in some interesting way. These three time signatures are very popular.
With time and practice, you will also go through additional educational sources. When you start learning about different genres, you will learn that some genres like some meters more than others.
Rhythm is connected with the emotions, and it is your job as a composer to make sure your rhythm conveys the ideas you want in the way you want. We may generalize that slow rhythm is sad, and fast rhythm is happy, but a skilled composer could work miracles the other way around.
Rhythm Variations
Rhythm can be exactly the same in every bar throughout the entire musical piece. But you can also introduce variations to your rhythm. This change can occur every bar, every two bars, every three bars. Rhythm can create sections made of a few bars that then repeat each other. From time to time you can introduce an unexpected change in rhythm (this is the syncopation I mentioned earlier) just to make things more interesting.
This way, you build rhythmic phrases.
You can change your rhythm by skipping a note.
Or by moving one note to another bar.
You can move an entire rhythmic phrase a beat to the right or to the left (to start it earlier or later). All of this is syncopation in practice.
Melodies, chords and rhythms strongly depend on the genre and the form. In EDM, rhythm is often exactly the same throughout the entire song, with some syncopations and small changes here and there. In popular music, sections of music (verse, chorus and so on) repeat a couple of times, so we also deal with sections of rhythm repeating in some manner. In film and game scores, rhythm (as well as melodies and harmonic material) can change a lot to fit what’s happening on the screen.
Melodies and rhythms are interconnected. Not every melody will work with a given rhythm, and not every rhythm will fit a given melody. You need to develop these two at the same time.
In the end, you need to listen to your creations and hear if they make musical sense.
***
All of this may seem quite complicated. You can develop the motif first. Or develop the rhythm first. Or decide the tempo first. There is no „right way” to create melodies and compose music - usually there are many elements that come together to create a composition, and every composer has a unique way of creating music.
All right, now let’s say a couple of things before we move on. Motifs, when mixed with additional notes, are used to create melodies that in turn create musical phrases. A phrase is usually two, or four bars long. (But you don’t have to use motifs if you don’t want to.)
A phrase should end with a cadence - for example, a deceptive cadence between after the first phrase, and a final cadence at the end of the last phrase. Two of such phrases create a musical period that should end with a final cadence.
Musical periods can be used to create a form - for example, the A section (like a verse) and B section (like a chorus). Between these periods we can add bridges and/or transitional elements. Before all of this we add introduction, and at the very end of all of this we create an outro. And thus, a song is born.
Let’s discuss all these terms mentioned above.
Melodic Phrase
A melodic phrase is a set of notes. Like the name suggests, it’s a phrase, and thus it’s longer than a motif. It can be a couple of bars/measures in length. The most common length is four bars long.

A good melody needs to make musical sense. It cannot be a random collection of notes played at a random time. It needs a sense, a structure.
Melody is never complete without a rhythm. In other words, rhythm provides an element of melody’ structure. Now you can take what you have learned about selecting a key, and scale, and creating a motif and developing the rhythm, to create a musical phrase.

Variations
Music is often based on tension and release - some parts of the piece create tension, other parts create release. Cadences - the chord movement that finishes the song or part of the song - are a good example of this (some cadences create tension, other cadences provide release). Often, breaks in music (between sections, musical periods) are also meant to create tension.
We work between tension and release to make sure our music is interesting. We want our listeners to be interested in our music. If we would repeat the same thing over and over again, we would lose this interest. We can keep this interest by introducing change, or variations, to our music. This can be achieved by changing the instruments, shifting the notes, changing the rhythm and so on.
Melody can be safely repeated, if the next repetition of this musical phrase is played on a different instrument. Thus, play the first repetition on one instrument, and then let it pass to another instrument. This will work nicely for solo sections, as well as in case of entire orchestras.
Another way to do this is to divide the phrase into a couple of sections, and then play each section on a different instrument, like it’s a form of dialog when two people are talking.

Here’s another tip: save everything - every motif, melody, phrase, chord progression and so on. You never know when the inspiration will come, or when you will use this material. Save short musical material as drafts in MIDI format on your hard drive.
Musical Period
Finally, we have a musical period, and it can be 4, 8, 16 bars/measures in length, or even longer. This can also be called a “part”.
A period (or part) is made of motifs, phrases and other filling elements. And now something important: later on we will discuss musical forms. Forms are often described with capital letters, such as A, ABAB, ABCBA and so on. Each of these letters usually represent a period (part), a complete musical sentence.
Anyway, your musical period:
Can be made of two musical phrases repeated one after another.
Made of a couple of musical phrases that have some variation to them, either note-wise, or instrument-wise.
Sometimes, the structure of your musical period and musical phrase is quite clear - some genres require this, like verse and chorus in pop music. EDM music is often very clear when it comes to its structure, while film music can be all over the place in order to fit what’s happening on the screen.
When composing music, you may wonder: how many motifs, phrases and periods should you use? That all depends on your own style and the goal of your music. Popular music will use simple motifs and short phrases that fit the verse/chorus form. Film scores will use complex ideas either with or without leitmotifs to create elaborate themes. Some composers create complex melodic lines that flow from one period to another. Other composers prefer short phrases and a lot of rhythmic elements. It all depends on the need, the form, the genre and your own personal style that will develop over time.
Remember that I’m describing here some musical elements such as notes and chords, and motifs and periods. But how you put them all together is your own choice. This is another moment when I want to emphasize: listen to a lot of music in the genre in which you want to compose. Listen for motifs, phrases, periods and bridges connecting all these together. Try to analyze how other composers make their music. Then try to copy them, and add your own elements, and thus develop your own style.
It is very important for a composer to listen to a lot of music and study a lot of music - analyze the structure, the motifs used, the parts, the instrumentation - whatever the genre. This is a good way to learn new things and improve your skills.
By the way, don’t forget about the fact that silence is often a part of music - don’t forget about breaks and quieter parts.

Now you know some basics about creating melodies, motifs, musical phrases and periods. With these basic rules and techniques you can go composing. The more you know about music and the more you compose, the easier it becomes to break the rules.
Secondary Themes
With the previous sections you’ve learned how to develop your main melody, phrases and main musical period - let’s say it’s your A section (you will learn more about musical form later). To simplify things, let’s say we’ve developed our verse, it’s done and we call it A.
Now we need to develop our secondary theme, the B section, or chorus if you like. Basically, you develop this theme the same way you developed the first one. You can use motifs, melodies and phrases to develop a new musical period.
Technically, you can do this in any way you want. But there are some guidelines to help you start (some of the things you can use haven’t been mentioned yet - they’re explained later).
You can take the motif or melody, and then use motif/melody development techniques to come up with a new melodic phrase.
You can change the chord progression for your secondary section.
You can introduce modulation to this secondary section.
You can try some contrast. For example, if the first section is fast, the secondary section could be slow.
The amount of contrast between two periods/themes/sections of your piece depends entirely on what you want to do as a composer. You can introduce a lot of changes and make the secondary theme completely different. Or you can use moderate changes, and make the secondary theme somehow different, but with recognizable, common elements with the first section, to make sure the entire piece sounds coherent.
Composing in a DAW
There is a great program for music notation and it is free: MuseScore. You can download it for free here:https://musescore.org.
It is used by many people around the world to compose music and even orchestrate entire pieces for a symphonic orchestra. If you are into standard music notation, you may want to check it out. That said, because we’re going to focus on composing directly in a DAW. So how do you compose music in a Digital Audio Workstation?
What I like to do is to create a single track I call either “DRAFT” or “COMPOSITION”, load a piano virtual instrument for this track and compose my motifs, entire sections or even entire piece of music on this track. Sometimes, I create multiple midi items (boxes) in my DAW on multiple tracks just to try different ideas, or try different fragments of the piece. I can then move these midi items around, slice them or glue them together.
Some people like to create separate tracks for a melody, countermelody, harmony and bass line.
Composing with Instruments
My personal preferences for composing are as follows: I create melodies and harmonies with a piano instrument (in DAW), and then I start orchestrating everything as soon as possible, developing both the melodic and harmonic material as well as the orchestration at the same time. But this is my personal style.
The best way to compose is to use a piano instrument, because a piano composition shows you all the imperfections and clarity that is very important in the composition process. But if you want, you can compose with other instruments if it’s easier for you, or if it inspires you more.
You can also use drums when composing to create rhythmic lines. Often I can’t get the feel of the rhythm with a piano alone, so I like to load drum libraries at an early stage of composition.
Actually, at some point you will decide that a mere piano is not enough and you want to actually write your music for various instruments. At this point you will be able to use your composition track as a reference track. With it, you can create many other tracks in your DAW, load various instruments, and start deciding on instrumentation - which note of your composition should be played by which instrument.
This is when the fun begins. Basically, some instruments can play an entire chord; some can only play the lead melody; some can only play the bass line; some instruments can play short notes and some can play long notes. Learn your genre first. For example, if you want to create trance, you learn about trance, thus you know this genre has some popular techniques of creating bass lines. Thus you know that you can add a bass virtual instrument to your composition, and make it play 2 or 3 quick notes on the weak beat on the root note of the chord in the chord progression.
But if you want to make orchestral music, you learn about it and you figure out your bass line should be based on the root notes and it can be played as single notes by a double basses doubled by bass trombone. And the main melody can be played by French Horn doubled by high violins.
Here in this book I cannot tell you what to do, because every genre has its own rules for instrumentation and orchestration and has its own unique instruments and sounds. But know this: for a lot of people composition is not separate from instrumentation. Basic Harmony
Basic harmony can be created with two notes - and they don’t have to be two different notes, they can be the same note in two different octaves. One of the most basic harmonies is using a drone. A drone is a single, constant note, around which the melody is built.
Figure 9.1 - A drone-based harmony.
Listen to my “Desert Winds” piece here:https://www.jamendo.com/track/1712206/desert-winds-emotional-duduk- it uses this simple harmony with a drone note playing in the background.
But in most cases, music uses more complex harmonies. But before we learn about them, we have to learn what voicing is and how we write with four voices (so called four parts writing ).
Voicing
There are four voices:
Soprano - the highest voice, and the highest notes.
Alto - a higher voice, with rather high notes.
Tenor - a lower voice, with rather lower notes.
Bass - the lowest voice, with very low notes.
When we talk about these four voices, we’re talking about so-called “four parts writing”. Four-parts writing is simply writing our composition in such a way that includes all these four voices. Each voice is represented by a note. Think of it this way: when you’ve learned about triads - basic chords - you’ve learned about chords made of three notes, and thus three voices. Double one of these voices, for example the root note, and there you have four voices. Now we can do a couple of things with these four voices.
Voices and Registers
Take a look at a piano roll in your MIDI editor. You can see as the octaves repeat themselves - these are registers. Generally, from C0 to around C2 we have the bass voice; from C2 to C3 we have the tenor; around C4 and C5 we have the alto, and above C5 we have the soprano. Thus, four voices - four groups of registers/octaves.
Open and Close Voicing
I explained this in the first part of this book, but I want to repeat it. If the notes are very close to each other vertically, we call this a close voicing. When the notes are far away from each other vertically, we call this an open voicing.
The general rule says that you should use open voicing in lower registers, and close voicing in higher registers. So the lower you play, the less dense the notes are; the higher you play, the more dense the notes are. We do this to avoid muddiness in the lower register and to create a balanced chord. Beginners should follow these rules:
In octaves C0-C2 : try to place notes an octave apart - a single note per octave should be fine.
In octave C3 : try to use no more than 2-3 notes.
In octaves C4 and above : use from 3 to more notes.
While these are general and basic rules, they’re great for beginners who haven’t learned how to put their balanced chords together. And creating balanced chords is a matter of genre and instrumentation for this genre, so you have to study the particular genre and its instruments to get this right.
Voice Movement
Up to this moment you’ve learned about the voices for a single chord - but what if you want to move forward to another chord, like in chord transitions? Well, the voices - the notes of the chords - need to move - this is called “ voice movement” and the voices can move in a couple of ways.
First, there is a similar motion (also known as direct motion), when voices move in the same direction, but not the same interval.
Figure 9.2 - Similar Motion.
When the voices move in a similar direction and the same interval, this is called parallel motion.
Figure 9.3 - Parallel Motion.
One of the voices may not move at all and remain on the same note, but another voice can move in either direction. This is called oblique motion.
Figure 9.4 - Oblique Motion.
Finally, when the two voices move to opposite directions (any opposite direction, both further apart or closer to each other), this is called contrary motion.
Figure 9.5 - Contrary Motion.
Between two chords, voices can move in many ways. Some voices/notes may choose similar motion, others may choose oblique motion, and others can choose contrary motion. You’re not limited to moving all four voices in the same direction. You can, but you don’t have to. But that’s not all.
In chord progressions, it is recommended that the chords should move in the smallest possible intervals. Take a look at the figure 9.6 below.
Figure 9.6 - A simple chord progression, but with bad voicing.
We made a transition from C Major to G Major. It’s OK to use this kind of chord progression if it sounds good and if it fits your musical idea, but it’s obvious our three voices have moved a lot - C went to G, E went to B and G went to D - these are huge intervals.
Instead, we should move our voices in smaller intervals, like in the example below.
Figure 9.7 - Voice movement between two chords, it sounds a lot better.
C went down to B. E went to D, and G remained unchanged. Our voices moved only a little, and yet we still moved from C Major to G Major, this time inverted. Simply try not to jump all around the MIDI editor.
An exception from this rule is the bass line - the bass line can use larger intervals such as fifth or fourth and this will be fine. And of course, the melody line can jump around if you want. But the harmony should usually use very restricted voice movement.
How to apply this? Well, you can follow this rule during the composition phase, and when creating individual melodic and instrument lines that are part of the harmony. When composing, try to stick to small intervals when moving voices. Every voice (every note of the chord) is an individual that can move to another note or stay on the same note if it likes. When writing for instruments, stick to the same rule - don’t make the instrument jump too large intervals for its part.
Parallel Fifths and Parallel Octaves
There are some traps when creating harmony and writing chord progression along with voice movement. One of such traps is called “parallel fifths” and “parallel octaves”. To simplify things (and use less words), please assume that the same things I’m about to say about parallel fifths also applies to parallel octaves.
A parallel fifth/octave occurs when two or more voices move in the same direction by the same interval equal to a perfect fifth or an octave.
For example, if out of four voices, the alto voice is the only one that moves up a fifth in the next chord, everything is fine. But if two or more voices, like an alto and tenor, both move up a fifth in the next chord, we have a parallel fifth. For an amateur, this doesn’t matter. But in reality, a parallel fifth weakens the harmony. Harmony sounds better, more “full”, if there are no parallel fifths or octaves occuring.
Take a look at figure 9.8.
Figure 9.8 - Voicing with and without parallel fifths (different registers).
The transition from the first chord to the second contains 3 parallel fifths:
Soprano: E to B
Alto: C to G
Tenor: A to E
But the second pair of chords (transition from third to fourth) contains no parallel fifths. I simply changed the voice movement. E still transitions to B. But C transitions to E now, and A transitions to G. This entire chord could use better harmonization though, because the spread of the notes is unfortunate. Figure 9.9 shows a better voicing.
Figure 9.9 - Proper voice movement with better voicing (different registers).
Notes are well-spread over four octaves. There are no parallel fifths. The root notes are doubled. I say this is a nice, although basic chord progression.
This entire rule applies not just for octaves and fifths, but also to parallel primes - avoid these as well. When writing harmonies, just go back from time to time and try to notice if you wrote some parallel fifths or octaves. If you did, change the voicing of the chords to avoid them.
More on Harmonizing the Song
Music creates a harmonious, coherent structure. By now, you’ve learned a lot. You know how to compose a melody, and you know what chords are. But how do you put your knowledge of melodies and chords together? How to harmonize the melody with chords?
Basically, you should think in the following way: you compose the melody first. Then, you need to compose the second melody, which is the bass line. Or, you can do this the other way around and compose the bass line first, and then the melody second. Usually, when you look at the chord, the fifth of the chord - the highest note - should be the note of the leading melody, that represents the upper part of the sound spectrum.
The bass note, the root note, should be the note of the bass line, representing the low end of the spectrum. The melody uses the upper notes of the chord, and the bass line uses the lower notes of the chord. This is a general and basic guideline.
This way, you already have two melodies. You can then define the harmonic rhythm: at what places of the bars and at what moment you want to change the chord. In this place of your bar/beat, the individual melodies should meet at different notes that will imply the chord.
Fill everything in between with a counter melody, and you can create a piece of music that uses all three elements - melody, bass line and filling countermelody, creating chords.
You can also compose by creating a chord progression first, and then compose a melody and bass line based on these chords. The bass line can use similar notes to the melody, too. And all three elements - melody, bass line, filler - will utilize the voice movement techniques mentioned earlier - so the melodies can move in any direction.
I have composed a simple melody in one of the previous sections. It’s time to harmonize it. Open the “harmonization.MID ” file.
Usually, the notes of the melody are the highest note of the chord - our brain hears the highest note more than it hears lower notes, so when you create harmony for your melodies, try to make the melody notes the highest notes of the chords.
Composing from chords is simple, but harmonizing a melody may be a bit more difficult. Fortunately, you already have a lot of necessary tools. Look at the melody below.

The Bass Line
The bass line is the lowest melodic line in your composition. It is provided by the lowest instruments, and in itself provides a foundation for the harmony and chords. When you harmonize your piece, you can add entire and complete chords to your music. But when needed, you can omit some of the notes of the chords, and simply use only two notes of a chord - one note for the melody, and second note for the bass - thus only implying a chord, never making it complete.
In such a way, the bass line complements the melodic line, it supports the melody.
Bass lines can be quite simple, with low instruments playing the root notes of the chords. Let’s take a look at some examples. (By the way, bass lines can use non-root notes, as long as your music sounds good to you.)

Harmonizing the Melody
You already know the diatonic notes you can use. The bass line doesn’t have to be built upon the root notes of the chords - you can use inversions. You can create a bass line that sounds good - either consonant or dissonant, however you like it.

But what chords should we use? There are three things to keep in mind. First, we need to decide the harmonic rhythm.
First, harmonic rhythm is a fancy term that tells us how often the chords change - they can change once a bar. A single chord can be two bars long, or a single bar can contain two chords. The chords don’t have to have the same lengths, as well. You already know this, because I talked about harmonic rhythm in a chapter about chords.

Second, let’s take a look at the notes in each bar - they can already suggest some chords. In the first bar we have notes E, B, C# and D. In this book we’re focusing on tonal music. This is a fancy term that tells us our music has a tonal center - a single note that we also call the key. When you choose a scale and a key for your piece, you already know the tonal center. I have chosen the E Minor scale, and thus I know the note E is my tonal center. Around this note three chords are built: I, IV and V - as already mentioned earlier. These are E Minor, A Minor and B Minor. All these three chords contain note E. Our music should be built upon this tonal center - this particular note should be “felt” throughout the song.
Thus, at least two chords should be used in this piece - I and V. If you also add chord IV, you’re awesome! You can put these chords in strategic places, where they make musical sense. Often, chord I is described as the point of origin. Chord IV is a “departure” and chord V is an “arrival”. Thus, our classic chord progression of I-IV-V-I is made. So by looking at your melody notes and bass line, try to figure out where you can put these three main chords, keeping in mind everything you’ve learned about chord progressions and cadences earlier in this book.
There are some nice chord progressions already mentioned:
I – IV – V - I
VI – IV – I – V
I – IV – VI – V
Vi - V - IV - V
ii - VI - I - V
You can use these chord progressions as a starting point for your own compositions. Use them in this exact form, or modify them however you want. As you may have noticed in this short list of progressions above, the chord progression doesn’t have to start with the I chord. It can actually start with chords IV or some of its substitute, as well. This is because chord IV is often described as „suggesting departure”. You shouldn’t use chord V to start a chord progression, as it doesn’t have the same feel - it suggests „arrival”. But at the same time, you can bend music rules - if starting a chord progression with chord V fits your musical idea, go for it.
All these three things - harmonic rhythm, taking a look at the notes in accordance with this harmonic rhythm and bar structure, and keeping a tonal focus in mind - are used simultaneously. Keep all three tips in mind, and this will help you build chord progressions.
The more you write music, the better composer you become. It’s a matter of practice and experience. As your brain forms new neuron connections, and you become familiar with the sound of chords, building chord progressions that sound nice and make musical sense becomes easier. At first, these words are difficult to understand - but with experience, you will notice composing music becomes easier and easier simply because you become more familiar with the tools at your disposal.

Suspended Chords, 7, 9, 11 and 13 Chords
Beside basic triads and seventh chords, we also have suspended chords, and chords made of more notes of the scale: 9, 11 and 13 chords. In this chapter we will learn how to build them and how to use them.

To make your music more interesting, you can use suspended chords. There are two basic sustained chords: sus2 and sus4. Sus2 changes the third into the second step of the scale; sus4 changes third into the fourth step of the scale. Both chords like to be resolved into a normal chord.

Beginners often stick to the traditional triads in their music, ignoring the seventh chords, or even more complex chords that are more dissonant in nature. While some genres and forms prefer traditional triads and it’s OK to use only these simple chords, sometimes it’s necessary to use more complex chords. For example, film scores can benefit greatly from dissonant chords that create an uneasy feel, and don’t forget that ninths and sevenths are quite useful, too.
Using the 7th chords is quite simple - just add one more note to your triads in your chord progression. You have already learned about these 7th chords in the first part of this book. Now, let’s learn about 9ths and even bigger chords.
9, 11 and 13 Chords
Let’s learn about bigger chords now. The first thing I want you to realize is that bigger chords are an expansion of the seventh chord. This is very important.
Let’s start with a ninth chord. It’s a chord that theoretically is made of five notes. We simply add another third above the seventh.
Figure 9.22 - The triad, the 7th and the 9th.
Figure 9.22. above shows three chords. The left one is a C Major triad. The middle one is the C Major 7. And the one on the right is our ninth chord. As you can see, it is simply another triad added above the seventh. And when you take a closer look, this ninth note is actually a D - which is basically a second to the tonic note of C. Whenever you add another third, either minor or major (to fit the diatonic notes of the scale and key) above the seventh, you will get a ninth chord.
Thus, the formula for the ninth is, depending on the key:
Seventh + minor 3rd
Seventh + major 3rd

Eleventh adds a third to the ninth, and thirteenth adds third to the eleventh. The very same thing goes for minor scales so there’s no need for additional screenshots. Big Chords and Four Voices
Now you know we can build many chords from a single scale and some are called seventh chords, some are ninth chords, and there are even bigger chords made of even more notes, right? But we only have talked about four voices and four parts writing - what gives?
7th chords are quite easy - they are made of four notes, and we work with four voices in our music. But what about 9th chords and even bigger chords? Technically, with some instrumentation, you can use chords made of five, six or seven notes, because why not? Obviously, they will sound very, very dissonant.
But what if you want to use a quite dissonant chord, or even the nice-sounding 9th chord, while sticking to only four voices? The answer is simple: you omit some of the notes. For example, let’s take a look at how we can use the 9th chord.
While it’s OK to use five or six notes of a chord to make it sound really dissonant, it’s not always your goal or intention.
Thus, we can omit some of the notes and still create a dissonant chord. But which notes can we omit?
We cannot omit the root note, because the chord will lose its identity. The root must stay. In the ninth chord, we cannot omit the third, because the third also gives the chord its identity. And we cannot omit the seventh, because if we commit the seventh, the chord may not sound right. If you omit the seventh in a ninth chord, it won’t be a ninth chord, it will be a suspended 2nd chord.
Thus, we need the root, the third and the seventh. The obvious last note in our four part writing is the ninth note. And thus, we have our ninth chord that can be used in four parts writing.
In the case of the eleventh chord, we can also omit both the third and the fifth and this will fit our four voices. Another option is to omit the third and ninth. Thus, in this case we have two ways to voice this particular chord.
In the case of the thirteenth chord, we need at least three main notes: the root, the seventh and the thirteenth. Then, choose the final fourth note out of all other remaining notes of this big chord based on your own taste or goal.
Using these big chords is simple. You can use these chords as a substitute for basic triads. For example, if you have a III chord in your chord progression, you can use a III 13th, or replace VI with VI 9th.
Every chord can be inverted. Go back to the chapter about chord inversions - in it, you have learned that to invert the chord, you take the lowest bottom note and move it to the top of the chord, making it the highest, top note. The same goes for all chords. When you have a 13th chord, yet you are limited to four voices, you take the lowest note and move it to the top. This is the first inversion. Then you take the new lowest note and move it to the top, and this is the second inversion. Then you take the “new new” lowest note and move it to the top, and this is the third inversion. This goes for 7th, 9th, 11th and 13th chords.
Experiment with these “advanced” chords - play them on MIDI grid, and experiment with their voicing. The more you listen to chords, the easier it is to use them in your music.
Countermelody
Now that you know more about building melodies and harmonies, it’s time to talk about countermelodies, also known as secondary melodies. Countermelodies can either create a contrast to the main melody, thus filling the audio spectrum of the song, or they can supplement the melody. In either way, they need to “musically” support each other, instead of fighting each other - there should be no clash between these two melodies. Of course, some dissonance may be appropriate, but dissonance is something intentional - clash is when two melodies obviously won’t fit together, and this dissonance is also based on dissonant chords that you carefully placed in your track.
It’s time to introduce three new terms: monophonic texture; homophonic texture; and polyphonic texture.
Monophonic texture is music made of a single melodic line - nothing else. This single melodic line can be sung by a choir, or played by various instruments, all of them playing the same melody, the same notes. There are no chords here. Only a single melody, maybe doubled by other instruments in the same octave or different octaves, but still the notes are the same.
Figure 9.25 - Monophonic texture.
Homophonic texture is made of a single melody line with chords. When some instruments play the melody, and other instruments play an accompaniment made of chords, this is a homophonic texture. The simplicity of relation between the melody and chords make this kind of music simple to make, yet with emotional depth provided by chords.
Figure 9.26 - Homophonic texture.
Polyphonic texture is music made of multiple melodic lines. This is when true counterpoint is applied and this is a difficult form of music. Chords are present here - they can be heard when multiple melodies arrive at a strategic point (like a strong beat), but beyond that you can hear these melodies as being separate.
Figure 9.27 - Polyphonic Texture - with an arpeggio based on chords.
Figure 9.28 - Polyphonic Texture with three separate melodies - melody on the top, countermelody in the middle and bass line at the bottom.
When I tell you to create a melody, countermelody and a bass line, I’m asking you to create a polyphonic texture. But don’t worry too much - if you create a homophonic texture (melody and chords) and add a secondary melody to this, it will still sound good as long as you remember about balance and separation. Balance and separation can be achieved by using different audio volume on separate melody lines, or by using different instruments, and by placing the melodies in various registers (octaves).
Your music can use all three types of texture within a single composition. For example, the grand finale of your tune can use a polyphonic texture, but an introduction or a breakdown in the middle can use homophonic texture and only play a single line of melody with additional chords.
You don’t always have to use all the chords, and sometimes you don’t even have to use a chord at all, and you can play a simple polyphonic melody with one instrument, or a couple of instruments playing “in unison”, which means together.

Tracks 1, 2, 3 played together show a basic concept of melody, countermelody and chords playing together. Track 4 shows an example of homophonic texture, with melody and chords. And tracks 5 and 6 show an example of polyphonic textures.
Composing countermelody requires some counterpoint knowledge, but fortunately, the absolute basics aren’t that difficult. See below:
The tempo of the countermelody can be the opposite of the main melody. When the main melody is fast, the countermelody should be slow.
Counterpoint between two melodies should be based upon intervals.
Desired intervals should be placed on the strong beat.
When we talk about countermelody, we actually talk about at least two melodies that are played at the same time, or at least two melodies that intertwine: for example, when the first melody plays, the second melody rests, and when the first melody rests, the second melody picks up.
Beginners should not try to create more than two melodies at once. On the other hand, the main melody, countermelody and a separate bass line already create three melodic lines. So whenever you make a melody and bass line, you already work with two melodies.
Whatever you do, the best thing to remember when composing a couple of melodies with an intent of creating polyphonic texture is this: when one melody moves, the other melody rests. If you want all melodies to move, it’s best to read some books about counterpoint and learn from these, since counterpoint is a wide subject that goes beyond the purpose of this book.
Basically, try to compose three melodic lines:
The main melody.
The secondary melody (countermelody).
The bass line.
And compose them in such a way that they complement each other, support each other, and in strategic places such as strong beats, they create the intended chords through proper intervals.
The rhythmic structure of the secondary melody can be a multiplication of the rhythmic structure of the main melody. For example, when the main melody plays a single note for each bar, the countermelody may play two or three or four notes for each bar, every bar.
The relation between the notes of two melodies can be based on intervals - intervals can be consonant or dissonant. Depending on what you want to achieve with the mood of your music, you choose the intervals that will fit your goal. Thus, when composing, identify the strong beat in your rhythm. On this beat, make sure the intervals between two or more notes of the separate melodies are the ones you want - and they shape the emotions you desire.
The intervals that are to form chords and provide emotional depth of the music should occur on a strong beat.
While composing, remember that countermelody can differ in:
Rhythm - one melody can use long notes, another melody can use short notes. The underlying rhythm of the countermelody can also be different.
Register - the countermelody can be played in a different octave than the main melody.
Color - the countermelody can use different instruments to create a contrasting color to the main melody’s instruments.
Articulation - one melody can use sustain notes, and the other melody can use staccato, short notes.
And these are only a few simple examples.
These are some basic things you need to know to create countermelodies and more complex musical structures.
Modal Interchange
Earlier, I told you that if the melody doesn’t fit the chords, you may want to change the melody. But what if you don’t want to? What if the melody is great, and you really, really need to fit the chord to the melody? What if you want to add a chord that sounds great, but contains non-diatonic notes (notes outside the scale)? Is it possible or allowed?
Yes - it’s possible and it’s allowed. The solution is called modal interchange.
Modal interchange is a fancy term that basically tells us that we can borrow a chord from a parallel key without losing the tonal center of our musical piece. This borrowed chord can be non-diatonic, which basically means it can contain at least one note that isn’t part of the scale.
Beware! When you start replacing chords, you risk losing the tonality of your music. So how to pick up a chord like this to replace it? Well, it’s quite simple, actually.
In the first part of this book, you have learned about the existence of relative keys - I mention this now to tell you that relative is not parallel - we’re talking about two different concepts here. A relative key has all the same notes to the original key, but a different root. For example, A Minor is the relative minor of C Major - the first one has a root on A, and the second one has a root on C. In total, they share all the same notes.
But the parallel key shares different notes, but the same root. For example, a parallel minor to C Major is C Minor. The notes of these two scales are different, but the root, the tonic note, is the same - note C.
When you want to use a parallel chord, you need to use a parallel key. Take a look at a figure below, 9.29.
Figure 9.29
The figure 9.29 above shows a simple chord progression I-IV-vi-V. For this example, I want to use a parallel key to replace my IV chord. This original chord progression is written in C Major scale, so I need to use the C Minor scale now. I want to change the IV chord - so I take a look at C Minor scale, I find the vi chord in there, and use this chord instead.
Figure 9.30
As you can see on the figure 9.30 above, the second chord is now the iv chord. Instead of the F Major chord, I use the F Minor chord now. This is modal interchange.
To use the modal interchange, take the same chord but from the parallel key to the key you’re using.
In order not to lose the sense of tonality, you should never change the tonic chord - I. This tonic chord should always remain the same to make sure the key of your music is well established.
Basically, you can always use a parallel minor to major, and parallel major to minor. But don’t forget you also have all these fascinating modes - Aeolian, Ionian, Phrygian, Dorian and so on - thus, you can use them as well.
For example, you write your song in C Major, and you want to replace the VI chord with a chord from C Dorian (just an example). Thus, you replace the original VI chord with vi-diminished chord, since this is the sixth chord in C Dorian (Dorian mode in the key of C).
To summarize - first decide the parallel key - either minor, major or one of the modes. Then, locate the chord built on the same step of the scale you want to replace. Finally, replace the chord. And never replace the tonic chord.

Decorations, Ornaments, Fillers
By now you know a lot about main and secondary melodies, bass lines and polyphonic textures. But sometimes you may end up with a musical piece that in some place remains silent and empty. This may be intentional and that’s OK. But if it’s empty and silent by mistake, you can fix it.
When the melody rests (meaning, the melody isn’t played), you can fill this empty space with silence - this sounds weird, but I simply mean you can play no notes and no sounds if this is what your composition requires. But sometimes it’s simply an irritating blank space that needs filling. For example in a dynamic piece of music, when the melody rests, you can introduce some kind of filler - a decorative element that will fill this empty space. Basically, there are FX fillers, and melodic filers.
Melodic fillers are simple melodic elements that are meant to fill the empty space.
For example, you may notice an “empty” space in the middle of your melody and you want to fill this space. This is a simple element like:
Very short melodic movement - a couple of notes based on chords that sounds good, or some kind of modified motif.
Figure 9.31 - The melody rests - a single note has been played, and I can use this empty space to add two additional melodic fillers.
A run - often used in orchestral music, a run is a very, very fast rising and then falling melody based on the notes of the chords, played on strings, trumpets or woodwinds, or any very dynamic, agile instrument.
Figure 9.32 - A run - it can be played quite fast on any instrument capable of agile playing.
Or you can use an FX filler:
A sound design element - a riser, a hit, or some kind of sound special effect.
An instrument special effect - like French Horn rip, or trumpet thrill and so on.
Obviously, every genre has its own types of fillers - always study your genre.
While melodic fillers can be explained simply as additional short melodies between the main parts of the main melody, both of these two FX fillers are genre-specific. When you make EDM, you will find many sound FX elements, usually in audio form, that can be simply inserted into the empty space of your track. And, as another example, when you make orchestral music, you learn about various “special sounds” every orchestral instrument can make, and thus you know when and how to insert them into your piece.
Modulation
Another thing often used by composers is modulation. You basically take all the notes of a piece of music and move them up or down.
To put it in wiser words, modulation occurs when at some point of the song we move the song into a different key of the same type. For example, we have a verse in C Major, and then we move the chorus to D Major - it’s still a Major key, but a note higher. We can also say that we change the tonic center of the song.
Listen to my song, The Brave One (https://www.jamendo.com/track/1661115/the-brave-one), which at 2:02 modulates up, but at 2:17 it modulates back to the original key.
You can modulate your song in many ways, but this book focuses on essentials for beginners, so let’s stick to modulating a semitone or tone apart, which is a simple way to use modulation.
Take a look at the figure 9.33 below.
Figure 9.33
In this case above, figure 9.33, I modulate the second part of the musical period (highlighted) a perfect fourth up, which means I move the part five semitones up. This is a nice modulation, because the new key shares the same notes with the previous key. The same situation would occur if I would modulate the piece by perfect fifth. Remember the wheel of fifths? Moving the key a perfect fourth or perfect fifth will get you to the next key, which shares all but one note with the previous key. This makes modulation very easy, because most notes are the same.
But you can also modulate the piece by a third or second, and this will sound OK as well. The best thing to do is to modulate by a whole step, not semistep. Basically, select all the notes in the given part you want to modulate and move all of them up or down. Modulating up will “add” energy to your song (for example, make it happier), and modulating down will “lower” the energy (for example, make it sadder), which can be used to change the emotional content of the song.
When can you use modulation? Whenever you want, of course - you’re the composer. But I’ll give you two simple examples. You can modulate only the grand finale of your song; or you can modulate your chorus in relation to the verse of the song, so the verse stays in one key and chorus is modulated to another key.
We have two ways to modulate our music:
Direct - we directly jump from one key to a new key without any kind of transition in between. As I said, the best way to do this is to move the key 1 or 2 semitones u p or dow n, or the third, or the second and so on. Anything bigger than this may require a lot more work and may be difficult for beginners (but not when you modulate by fourth or fifth, which has many notes in common). The best way is to move the key a whole step up or down. This is the easiest way.
With transition - the second type of modulation requires a transition element - this transition is built up with common chords. This means we can jump to a new key, if this new key shares at least one chord with the original key. Then, we can use this common chord to build our chord progression in such a way that we would utilize this common chord between two different keys. This transition will work even with only one common chord used between two different keys.
You know the key in which you compose your music and you know all the chords used in this key. If you want to modulate with transition, decide a key to which you want to modulate your music. Then take a look at the chords in this new key. Compare the chords of both keys and see if there is at least a single chord common - which is exactly the same in both keys, even if it serves a different function (for example, one is subdominant, other is tonic, but the notes are the same). Use this chord between the two different parts of your music as a transitional element - put it at the end of the first chord progression and before the new chord progression (because once you have modulated, nothing stops you from creating a new chord progression, but remember, too many changes may cause your new part to sound alien and unfamiliar). If there is no common chord between two keys, you may need to select a different key.
But if you really want that particular key to modulate into, you may need to create a complex transition through many keys in between - to figure out a path of common chords between 3 or 4 different keys until you land on the key you really want. This is the difficult part - so we won’t deal with it in this book, as it’s more advanced.
As a beginner, try to stick to modulating into the same kind of key (major or minor), a whole step apart, or by a perfect fourth or perfect fifth, which is very easy.
Summary
Let’s summarize:
Know the key your melody is in.
Based on your knowledge of the key and its notes, create a bass line that sounds good.
Know the chords used in your chosen key and what notes these chords are made of.
Know which chord is which - like which one is tonic I, which is dominant V and so on.
Look at the melody and decide for the harmonic rhythm - how the chords will change and when.
Look at the notes of the melody and the notes of the bass line, and choose which chords also contain these notes, or at least some of them.
Try these chords - if they sound good, leave them. If they don’t, change them.
When you do all of this, remember everything you’ve learned earlier: chord progressions and tips regarding these progressions, cadences, voice movement, parallel fifths and so on.
10. Music Composition - The Form
Now we have to put all these elements - motifs, melodies, chords - together into some kind of musical form.
Form is a blueprint for music. Some forms are described with letters, such as ABA. Other forms, more traditional, have their own names, like a “Rondo” or “Sonata”, or “12-bar blues” (which is basically a chord progression, but it can be used as a form). This blueprint generally tells us how to produce music in a given genre.
It would be quite difficult to explain forms and genres of music in a book like this. But there are some basic forms we can learn to create our music. For example, there’s an “AAA” form, in which a section of music is repeated over the entire song, or an “ABAB” form, with verses and choruses. In this chapter of the book we will take a look at various, yet basic forms. This way, you will be able to take the melodic and harmonic material you already composed, repeat it a couple of times, introduce changes and finally come up with a finished composition.
The best way to learn about forms is listening to music. Select a genre you want to write in, then find some tracks you enjoy and study them very carefully, try to find the beginning, the middle and the end. In some genres it’s very simple because the sections are clearly defined. In some other genres, this may be difficult.
Music tells a story. It’s true no matter the genre. And in every story there is a beginning, middle and end. You start with a beginning that sets the tone of the piece. You use the middle to tell the story, and you use the ending to finish the story. This approach works whether it’s a song with lyrics, an instrumental score fitting a film, or a club EDM tune. The length and form of the story changes, just like we have various story forms: poems, novels and so on.
So what forms are out there? I won’t list all of them, but you can do a lot with basic forms. To simplify things, instead of “period” I’m going to use the word “part”, which is basically the same thing. Every part contains a chord progression and melody (or melodies) built upon them.
A - this form contains one part that is repeated a couple of times, thus resulting in AAA form.
AB - this is a binary form made of two parts, like a verse and a chorus. Often it uses the idea of one part being a question, the other part being an answer. This could result in AB form, or ABAB form, or AABB form. And each part can introduce some additional variations.
ABA - a song form, it’s slightly different from the AB form, because it ends with the A part.
ABCBA - an arch form has three different parts repeated in a, well, arch form.
ABACBCB - popular music likes to use this form.
We also have some classical forms, and two of them are quite popular.
Sonata - it’s similar to ABA form. The first A part presents the main theme, along with additional minor elements or smaller themes. The B part, development of the song, may sound very differently, using different key signatures and different time signatures. The last A repeats the A part, perhaps with some variations.
Rondo - there are two types of this form. The five-part rondo and the seven-part rondo. Five-part rondo is ABACA and the seven-part rondo is ABACADA. The A part is the only thing that stays the same, with some variations. And the B, C and D parts can be completely different. And I mean really, really different, compared to the A part.
And even if we talk about AB or AAA forms, these aren’t only periods (parts) repeated over and over again. In front of them, we can have a beginning, at the end we can have an ending, and between parts we can add breaks and transitions. Thus it becomes:
AAA - Intro A break A transition A Outro
AB - Intro A break B Outro
ABACBCB - Intro ABA transition CBCB Outro
So remember, between each part you can create transitions and so called “bridges” that connect two parts together.
By now you already know everything you need to know - how to build chords and melodies. All you have to do is to put these things together, like bricks, to make the desired creation. At first this may sound abstract and difficult, but like with everything else, give it enough practice and it won’t hold any secrets any more.
***
In any case, music requires two things: repetition, because the listener needs something familiar to focus upon; and contrast - something new, a change in the music that captures and holds the listener’s interest.
Adding repetition to your music is simple: just repeat the same part twice. But be careful, too many repetitions that sound exactly the same may get boring. You can also repeat the same part with some changes - for example, keep the chords and melody the same, but change the instruments.
Contrast can be achieved in various ways.
You can achieve contrast by using chord progression - dissonant chords create tension that can be resolved in consonant chords. Between them we notice the contrast.
You can use contrasting themes and sections, such as A and B, that are contrasting to each other.
You can repeat the same section, but achieve contrast by introducing a completely different instrumentation.

How to Start a Song
Starting a song (and ending it) can be quite problematic because we often don’t know where to start.
You can start your piece by playing a drone (single root note) and a melody above it. My track, “There is Hope” begins in this way, with strings playing a drone note constantly, and piano playing a melody on top of the strings.

You don’t really build up the instrumentation, you just use different lyrics and change the instrumentation between verse, chorus and breakdown.

How to End a Song
All right, you know you can end a song with a cadence, like V chord followed by I. But that’s just a cadence - is there more to it? Here are some ideas to end a song.
You can end a song abruptly - play the final tonic chord and end the song just like that. “Amara” ends like this.
You can end up a song with a new short melody acting as a melodic decoration based on the tonic chord. “Storm Rising” ends like this.
You can end the song with final cadence, and then repeat the main melody in a very gentle fashion, with the melody fading out. “There is Hope” ends like this, repeating the melody twice, first on Cellos, then on piano and fading out.
You can play and repeat the final chorus or period and simply lower the volume to complete silence. This is how many old-school songs used to end.
You can introduce a new final melody that would make the song feel “complete”, like an epilogue of a book.
There’s not much to tell about form beyond this. It’s more like a matter of genre and your own invention.
You should not ignore the fact that we have access to a lot of music in every genre imaginable. It’s OK to be inspired by songs and music composed by others. If you can’t figure out how to start a song, how to end it, or how to create interesting breaks and transitions from one period to another, listen to the songs representing your genre and learn from them.
Study how they create intros, and endings. Study what they do with the middle. What are the rules of the genre? What are the suggestions for this genre? We always have at least an intro, an A period, and an outro. What we do with these is often genre-specific.
Creating Breaks and Transitions
A “break”, “breakdown” or a “transition” or “bridge” are terms we use to describe additional musical elements between the main sections of your music. A guitar solo can be an example of such a break. Another example of a break is a one or two bars between EDM sections just to add a snare roll for tension.
Breaks may stand out from normal chord progression of the song, or be based on it.
How to create transitions or bridges? Well, in any way you want. You can:
Create a short chord progression that would bridge between two parts and create a simple melody connecting them together.
Create a dramatic pause between two parts.
Use only one chord with some sound effects between two parts.
Prolong the final chord of the part and slightly expand the melody of the previous part, connecting it with the beginning of the melody of the next part.
Introduce a short, different melody between two well established parts.
These are only some of the ideas.
Dynamics
It’s rather a unique approach, but now is a good time to talk about the dynamics. Dynamics is a term that is used to describe how loud the instrument is. You cannot compose your music at the same level of dynamics. Some parts should be loud, some should be quiet. Some should fade out, and some should fade in. Normally, in musical composition and with traditional notation, we use a couple of words that describe the dynamics: how loud an instrument is played, how “strong” it sounds. These are:
Pianississimo - ppp - Very, very soft.
Pianissimo - pp - Very soft.
Piano - p - Soft.
Mezzo piano - mp - Moderate soft.
Mezzo forte - mf - Moderate loud.
Forte - f - Loud.
Fortissimo - ff - Very loud.
Fortississimo - fff - Very, very loud.
Learn these words, their abbreviations and what they mean, because most books and courses in music use these words. These terms, as well as words crescendo and diminuendo that you will learn in just a moment are also present in virtual instrument’ manuals and interfaces.
Every instrument - whether it’s an acoustic guitar, a traditional French Horn, or an electronic synth lead - has its own dynamic range. Some instruments can play anywhere from ppp up to fff. Other instruments have a limited range, and may not be able to play in all dynamics. Instruments also change how they sound (their timbre changes ) depending on the dynamics. This is a subject for instrumentation and orchestration, so I won’t get into details.

There are also two words that stand for fading in and fading out of sound:

While we’re at dynamics, I want to introduce you to one more term - legato - since you will encounter it quite often. Legato has two meanings.
Legato in traditional notation describes a “legato” - we mark some notes with a curved line to tell the player this small part of music should be played smoothly, and the notes should feel connected.

But in digital music production, in a DAW, legato has a slightly different meaning and it’s important to understand the difference. It still means the notes should sound connected, but in the case of DAW and MIDI editors, legato actually points us to a very practical technique.

Legato is offered mainly by most commercial libraries of instruments that can be played in such manner in real life. Cheaper, or free libraries will not offer legato, and the transitions between the notes may not sound realistic.

Tips for a Good Start
Let’s start with a couple of tips.
Don’t spend too much money when you’re just starting out. You don’t need too many things to make music, and the things you do have don’t have to be the best on the market. The simplest example is this: for the first few months, or even a year, you will be learning how to compose and how to produce your music. At this time you may already start learning how to mix music, but you may not have too many great pieces to mix. There’s no need to treat your room with sound panels and bass traps. And you don’t have to buy an audio interface and studio monitors for the first couple of months.
Always check product reviews. This should be obvious. But even product reviews can be tricky. For example, there are many reviews of basic and cheapest Mackie studio monitors, but a lot of people say they can easily get broken. Thus, unfortunately, in the end you have to make the final decision. But still, read the reviews!
Expensive doesn’t mean better. You really can get great gear and hardware that is also affordable. At the same time, expensive doesn’t mean stuff won’t break. And in many ways, expensive gear often has many features and can do a lot of things you won’t really need at first. And you may never use them at all. Start small and buy affordably.
If you don’t know something, learn. This is exactly what you’re doing right now. The Internet is full of useful articles and tutorials, reviews and helpful discussion boards, where you can find the answers. This is actually how I figured out the type of cables I needed for my studio monitors :).
Never fall into the “hype” trap. Just because something is popular today and everyone buys it, doesn’t mean it’s good and you need it. This goes both for hardware and software. I have heard many pieces of music, even entire video game soundtracks, made with cheap equipment and completely free instruments and software.
It’s not the gear, it’s you. A great professional can do miracles with very little gear and software. A total amateur will waste his time even in the most expensive studio. Keep this in mind.
I always say, “start small” and build from there. The same goes for software for music production, too, not just hardware.
Headphones for Home Studio
Let’s assume that you won’t be recording any live instruments just yet (if ever). But even when you compose with virtual instruments, at some point you will have to mix your music. You can learn a lot about mixing music without any special headphones or speakers, so don’t rush things. But as soon as you decide you want to improve your mixes, you need some speakers or headphones that have a better quality of sound.
Which one to choose? The idea is quite simple: purchase open or semi-open headphones first. Why not studio speakers (studio monitors)? Because studio monitors require you to have a room that is prepared for work with audio - a room with sound panels, diffusers and bass traps. Thus, you need sound treatment such as bass traps or acoustic panels, and without them, studio monitors are pointless. At first, you want to lower your costs and you don’t want to buy any bass traps and such. Thus, headphones are a better choice.
There are many brands on the market, but I will keep things simple and recommend only one product: AKG K240. They’re affordable, they cost about $70 and they’re good enough to start with.
Open and semi-open headphones are great for mixing music (at the same time, close headphones are a better choice for recording) - you will see these terms (open, semi-open, close) in product description. And whenever you shop for headphones or speakers for mixing, you want a product with as flat sound as possible. Flat in this case means that the sound the headphones/speakers produce is equal throughout the frequency spectrum. Neither the bass, nor the midi or the highs is boosted. Boosted frequencies are not recommended when you want to use your gear for mixing. In many stores you should be able to ask the support whether the speaker or headphones have a flat spectrum of sound.
MIDI Keyboard
The next thing you may want to buy is a MIDI keyboard. Actually, this will be the first thing you get if you already know how to play a piano. If you do, getting a MIDI keyboard is a natural thing and there’s nothing more to say. But if you don’t know how to play a piano, MIDI keyboard will not be your priority.
You may buy it now, or even in the future, but I do say that you may find it useful. Having a MIDI keyboard allows you to play melodies and chords before putting them into your DAW’s project. If you can hear your musical ideas, it’s much easier to compose and develop them.
I recommend you get M-Audio Oxygen 61. This is a great MIDI keyboard that will work perfectly fine for years. But, please note – I’m not a professional piano player, and I don’t record my music live. If you want to play a lot, record live, and use the keyboard for live performances, Oxygen 61 may be OK, but I’m not sure how well it will work throughout years under “heavier” use. Audio Interface
After some time, when you already know how to compose music, you produced a couple of nice tracks and you want to improve your mixes, you may start by grabbing an audio interface. Audio interface is like a sound card with a lot of additional sockets and connectors. You will need it sooner or later in order to:
Connect your headphones that require an additional amplifier.
Connect your studio monitors that won’t have a cable that fits your PC.
Connect your microphone that isn’t USB-ready.
To start with, I would recommend Behringer U-Control audio interface. It is very affordable, and it allows you to connect stereo input (mic), stereo output (studio monitors) and headphones. It’s a basic setup that works on USB cable to connect with your computer.
Audio interface can also allow you to plug in additional instruments that require amplifiers, like guitars. They also can come with MIDI sockets, which is useful to connect a more advanced MIDI keyboard, or a physical hardware-based mixer. More advanced audio interfaces have special sockets that allow you to plug in hardware-based effects, such as compressors, equalizers or reverbs.
Once you have an audio interface, you can start thinking about buying studio monitors, but there’s a catch: room treatment.
Room Treatment
Before you start to think about recording instruments and mixing with studio monitors, you should think about room treatment. You need to lower the amount of echo in your room, and deal with some unwanted frequencies. We do so by putting a carpet on the floor, and gluing special panels to the walls, corners and the ceiling.
First, you may want to grab a carpet to place on the floor. This will reduce sound reflections.
Every corner of the room, that is: wall-to-wall and wall-to-ceiling, should be treated with bass traps. These absorption foams are used to deal with very low frequencies that like to build up in the corners of the room.
Walls should be covered with absorption panels. Such panels come in various sizes and shapes. Some are flat, some have a lot of pyramids one them :).
The simplest way to do this is as follows: put bass traps in wall-to-wall and wall-to-ceiling corners. Place a small carpet on the floor. And place absorption panels on all the walls and the ceiling. This is the best tactic for beginners. Try to cover at least 1/2 or 3/4 of each wall with foam panels.
If you want to set up a more professional studio, a good idea is to hire a professional sound specialist who will check your room and recommend more professional treatment, or at least go online and read a lot of websites about room treatment if you want to figure this one out by yourself. Every room is different – it has a different shape and size, so each room must be treated individually. Sound waves move in various ways through various rooms, and only an expert can really advise you as to what you really need and where to put it.
You can also place a diffuser (diffusion panel) on the wall, but again this is a more complex stuff and you should ask an expert.
While some online tutorials suggest you can use blankets on the walls and a lot of pillows, don’t do this. This will work well if you’re recording YouTube videos or online courses, but it won’t be very useful for mixing music. Also, don’t even think about buying egg boxes (egg cartons). They really don’t work :).

Buying studio monitors may be a bit more complicated, because you also need pads, stands and cables.

If you don’t have an audio interface that allows you to plug in passive speakers, buy only active speakers. Loops and Premade Sounds
You can make music by typing notes into the MIDI editor and playing these notes with virtual instruments. But, there’s another way, you can also use premade sounds. For example, you can use drum loops - these are short audio files recorded in specific rhythm and tempo (usually given in the file’s name), that you can add to your composition (like “drag and drop”) and these audio files will loop when properly configured or pasted one after another. Instead of building your own drum loop from various instruments, you can simply add a piece of WAV file and be done with it.
FX sounds are another example - risers, hits, drops, genre-specific special effects - entire companies have been created that offer this kind of thing.
Loopmasters -https://www.loopmasters.com- is an example of a store where you can buy packages with various sounds to use in your music. You can also find free sounds for various genres.
Here’s something important: when you use these kinds of pre-recorded phrases or loops, make sure to wrap them with additional sounds - do not use pre-recorded files “stand alone”. For example, add a pad or chords to your drum loop, and don’t use vocal phrases in a void, with no additional music playing at the same time. This is due to copyright scanners.
Here’s how it works: when you purchase audio packs to use in your music, you get the right to use them commercially. But published music, for example on YouTube, is being scanned by software that looks for copyright infringement. This software does this by comparing waveforms to what they store in their database. When someone uses a WAV sample in their music and has submitted this piece of music to, for example, YouTube Content ID program, once you publish your music with the same WAV sample, it may be marked as an infringement and blocked, even if you have the right to use this sample commercially because you bought the license. Thus, people use these samples on top of additional musical elements: pads, chords, countermelodies and so on. This way, the waveform changes and isn’t easily recognized by automated software.
(This solution isn’t perfect, so it can be tricky to use pre-made samples in music.)
So even if you have the license to use something commercially, you still need to know how to use it. This is a big drawback of pre-made samples. Whenever possible, try to create your own FX and sounds.
Still, using pre-made loops and samples can be either beneficial (it speeds up the production and makes your life simpler), or it’s simply preferred by some producers. Or, it can be simply a great way to start while you’re still a beginner.
Mixing and Effects Plugins
When a composer is done with creating hers or his composition, or the band is done recording, all these separate instrument and audio tracks have to be made whole - we say they need to be mixed together. Mixing is a process of post-production, when you clean and modify individual audio tracks in such a way that they sound well together. Most of this cleaning and modification is done through various plugins and effects.
When you get into mixing and producing music, at some point you may need additional plugins: a better equalizer and compressor, a professional reverb and so on. What’s With the Presets?
What are presets? A preset is a ready-made setting either for a plugin or an instrument. For example, both a mixing software and a synth can have presets. A synth preset will generate a nice sound according to its settings; a mixing plugin preset will change the sound according to its settings.
It’s OK to use presets - for mixing plugins and instruments - if you’re a beginner. But presets can get you only so far. At some point you will have to learn how to use the plugins you’re working with, because presets work only in a limited way. For example, an equalizer preset for cleaning the low end frequencies will not work properly if you don’t know what low end is and how to find it in your unique instrument.
Consider presets more like a starting point - learn from them and build upon them.
Plugins and Effects
I mentioned you can buy plugins for mixing. Let’s learn what kind of plugins you can use.
Audio can be edited and modified live in every DAW. It doesn’t matter if you’re working on a pre-recorded audio file, or a sound generated “on the go” based on MIDI notes. What can we do with our sound? Well, we can stretch it, speed it up or slow it down. We can change the frequencies it contains. We can add an echo (delay) effect or reverb effect. Basically every single sound you hear in video games or films have been processed inside a DAW using various “effect plugins”.
What kind of effect plugins are there? There’s a couple of them. In this book I will simply mention some basic plugins and effects. Explaining each and every one of them goes beyond the scope of this book, but once you know the plugin exists, you can find tons of tutorials on the Web.
Equalizers
An equalizer is used to modify the frequency spectrum of a sound, and thus we can cut off some frequencies, or boost other frequencies. Equalizer can be used both as a sound shaping tool (like in some genres of music, or in sound design), but mainly it is used in mixing to clear the mix and make it sound good. For example, you can completely cut off the higher range of sound of a bass drum, to make sure nothing will clash with a guitar higher in pitch.

Expanders
They can be used to lower the volume of a signal that is below a specific threshold. De-essers
These are a form of compressor that lowers the volume of specific sibilant frequencies - all these nasty “ssss” and similar sounds our mouth makes when we speak. It’s often used on vocals or in any sort of speech recording.
Delay
A delay is a special effect. It repeats the sound over time - think of it as an echo effect. And here’s something very important:
Delay time should work along the beat as measured in milliseconds and this needs to be calculated. Basically, this means your reverb, delay or echo should “sound parallel” to the beat, unified in rhythm. Otherwise, you will get two different rhythms that will clash.
First, you need to calculate the beat in milliseconds.
You do so by dividing 60000 by the tempo. Let’s say your tempo is 120 BPM. Thus:
60000/120 = 500 milliseconds
If your tempo is 75, then:
60000/75 = 800 milliseconds
This 500 ms and 800 ms are the delay time. This value can then be passed to your delay plugin or any other effect that requires this setting. Thanks to this, the rhythm of the effect and the main rhythm of your song will be more or less similar, and won’t clash.
Reverb
Reverb adds a sense of space to your music - it does this by simulating sound reflections. With it, you can simulate the sense of a studio or even a concert hall. Reverb is used for sound design, but mainly it makes your instruments sound more real.
Delays and reverbs are used to add a sense of physical space to your tracks, and they can also be used creativity, to shape the mood and atmosphere of your instruments. For example, vocals with longer reverbs have a tendency to sound a lot more emotional.
***
Effects should be added to groups and individual tracks. Then, these tracks can be routed to the master track. It is usually recommended not to add any effects to your master track.
And as I mentioned already, most DAWs come with a set of stock plugins, so you don’t have to buy anything beyond what your DAW offers you.
Signal Chain
Consider audio and sound a signal.
It’s worth knowing that an audio signal travels a very specific path from the moment it is created to the moment it reaches the speakers. Take a look at the schematics below:
Figure 11.10
The first step in the signal chain is the track. Here you can add midi notes and audio files. This is the first step, the original source of the signal. The signal then travels to the master channel by default. All tracks converge within the master channel. But if you want, in most DAWs you can direct the signal (route it, and we tall this signal routing) differently, for example to a group track (group channel). Before the signal can reach the channel “above it”, at every “arrow” stage, you can add various effects - plugins such as equalizers or reverbs. These plugins are used to “process” the sound.
After processing, the signal finally reaches the master track (or master channel).
In most DAWs, there is a master track. Quite often, it’s not visible in the workspace area, but it’s present in the mixer window. This is the final destination of the sound. Every single track sends its data into this master track (or master channel), and from the master channel the sound is outputted to your speakers.
Consider the following figure 11.11:
Figure 11.11
Figure 11.11 above shows that most string tracks are grouped with “CS STRINGS” - all signal from strings tracks goes to this main group track. From it, the signal travels both to WET and DRY tracks - thus, the signal is doubled. It is then merged together again, because it travels from WET and DRY track to PREMASTER track. And from PREMASTER, the signal travels to the MASTER track, from which it goes to your speakers. On every single step of this journey, you can make changes by adding various effects and plugins.
While this knowledge isn’t necessary for learning music theory, sooner or later it will come in handy when you start producing your own music, because understanding signal chain is important in mixing and adding effects to your audio. For now, just be aware of the existence of a signal chain.
Monitoring Audio Levels
One more thing - every DAW has some kind of monitoring system - when you play an audio, this monitoring system displays dynamic columns that go up or down depending on the volume of the sound. Take a look at the figure 11.12 below.
Figure 11.12
On this figure 11.12, frame number 2. shows such a dynamic column, along with some digits. These digits represent decibel levels - the higher the column goes, the louder the sound. Decibel levels go from negative numbers, like -10 dB. The 0 dB level is the maximum level of the sound. Anything above that, like +2 dB, is called “clipping”. Below 0 dB, the columns are usually green - the sound is fine. On 0 dB, the column may display orange or yellow elements - a warning sign. Above 0 dB, monitoring will display red elements - and you’re “clipping”.
Clipping causes problems. First, it causes sound distortions. The sound coming from the speakers will be distorted, it will contain unpleasant “artifacts” that will ruin your music. Second, clipping causes too much power input on the speakers. This may physically damage your speakers. And that’s a problem, no matter if you’re using studio monitors that cost thousands of dollars, or speakers for a hundred dollars.
Avoid clipping - you can do this simply by lowering the volume of your track. But note this: separate tracks may not cause clipping, but when you stack multiple tracks together (guitar, piano, drums and vocals together, for example), the combined sound on the master channel (master track) may be clipped.
What I like to do is to compose my music on cheap PC speakers for 20-60 dollars, and only when I’m done with composition and I start mixing, I put a limiter on the master channel so that no clipping occurs, and then I turn on my studio monitors for mixing purposes.
The frame marked as 1 shows a panning in a mixer window, and the frame marked as 3 shows a volume slider, which is the same thing as a volume knob, it simply looks differently.
Effect Chain
Most DAWs recognize the concept of effect chain - the order in which the FX are applied. Earlier, I talked about the signal chain. But when you open the FX window of your single track, you may notice you can add many effects and plugins for this particular track. The order of these plugins is the effect chain - the signal is first processed through the very first plugin on the list, then goes to the next plugin, and the next, and the next, and finally it is released and sent further according to your routing setting (signal chain). Thus, every next plugin processes the signal already processed by the previous plugin.
There are general guidelines for adding effects and plugins. For example, let’s say you want to add an equalizer, compressor and reverb to your track and this is exactly the order you want to use. First, add an equalizer to modify the frequency of the sound. Then, add the compressor to compress the already eq-ed sound. Finally, add reverb to the processed signal at the end of the effect chain.

Rendering
Rendering is the process of exporting all your data into a final audio file - to put it simply, with rendering you create the final file ready for distribution.
A DAW has some sort of rendering window where you can change all the settings. In Reaper, you go to File -> Render.
Figure 11.16
A new window will appear.
Figure 11.17
In this window, you can set the source of your render and its bounds.
Figure 11.18
The source is usually the master mix - in this chase, every single track that isn’t muted (or routed in such a way that isn’t audible) will be rendered into the final file. You can also:
Render stems - the tracks you actually select for rendering with your mouse (in Reaper you select tracks by clicking them while holding down the CTRL or COMMAND button). This will result in tracks rendered as separate files.
Master + Stems - you will render both the main file, and each separate tracks.
Regions - selected regions within the workspace.
Selected items - only the pieces you actually select with the mouse (like a selected audio file within the workspace).
You can also set the bounds of the rendering:
Figure 11.19
Custom range will allow you to type the time range of the track you want to render. Entire project will render the entire project until no sound is generated. Time selection will render only the selected timeline range. And project regions will only render the, well, project regions.
You can define the directory and the name of the file to render. Directory is a folder on your hard drive where you want to render your file. In Reaper, you can set the name of the file in the “File Name” area.
Figure 11.20
Another useful thing a DAW can offer is wildcards (or some other system of naming files). In Reaper, wildcards can be typed into a file name, and during rendering, they will be replaced. For example, using the wildcard $track in file’s name will result in the file named after the track. If I have a track named “strings”, and I render the file as $track_mytrack.wav, it will result in a file named “strings_mytrack.wav”. This feature is useful when you want to render multiple files with very specific names generated automatically.
Figure 11.21
DAWs allow you to define a couple of settings for rendering. The basic settings are sample rate and number of channels. Beginners should stick to 44100 sample rate, and stereo channel settings.
You can render your files into various formats.
Figure 11.22
You can render your music to mp3 or ogg files for demo purposes, or as drafts for review, because these formats are small in size. But these formats compress your audio, thus lowering its quality.
For final products, stems, individual tracks for mixing etc., always work on uncompressed files, thus render to WAV files. Other uncompressed formats are AIFF and FLAC, but usually, WAV format in either 24 bits or 16 bits is an industry standard.
When all the settings are set, you hit “Render” and wait a bit. Bit projects can take up to a few minutes to render.
12. Summary
In this book you’ve learned the basics of music theory - the building blocks of music. Throughout the expanded edition, you also learned about music composition and some basics of music production. Right now all of this is still fresh to you. Don’t worry if you don’t understand everything just yet. Just open your DAW, start a new project and start composing, step by step.
As I said at the beginning, you may feel lost right now, but don’t worry - once you start learning music composition, everything will become much easier to understand. Once you know composition, the world of music is open to you.
It may take a few weeks or even a few months, maybe even a year or two, to become “fluent” in everything you’ve read in this book. Everyone learns at their own speed. The most important thing is practice. The more music you compose, even without finishing and releasing your tracks, the more you understand.
